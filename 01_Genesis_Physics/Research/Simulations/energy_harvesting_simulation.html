The Cochlea: Anatomy of a Living MRG

The inner ear contains a spiral-shaped, fluid-filled cavity called the cochlea (from the Greek κοχλίας, "snail"). Cross-section reveals a structure that maps precisely onto the Zone Architecture:

Cochlea Anatomy

What the ear actually is

Scala vestibuli (upper chamber) — filled with perilymph fluid, higher pressure. Transmits sound vibrations inward from the oval window.

Basilar membrane — a thin, stretched membrane running the length of the cochlea. Varies in width and stiffness (narrow/stiff at base, wide/flexible at apex). Vibrates in response to pressure from both sides.

Scala tympani (lower chamber) — filled with perilymph fluid, connected to scala vestibuli at the apex (helicotrema). Returns pressure waves back toward the round window.

Stria vascularis — a layer of specialized cells that actively pumps ions (K⁺) to maintain a +80 mV endocochlear potential across the membrane. This is an active, energy-consuming process. Without it, the system goes dead.

Outer hair cells (OHCs) — electromechanical amplifiers sitting ON the basilar membrane. They use the endocochlear potential to actively amplify membrane vibrations. They are motors, not just sensors.

Inner hair cells (IHCs) — the actual sensors. They convert membrane vibrations into electrical nerve signals sent to the brain. They harvest the vibration energy.
