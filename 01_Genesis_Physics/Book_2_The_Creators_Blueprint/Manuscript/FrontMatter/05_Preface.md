# Preface

**[DRAFT — for author review. Written in the canonical Jeff L. Raymond voice. Deliberately distinct from the Chapter 1 kitchen-table hook: this is the author framing the project; Chapter 1 puts the reader in the scene.]**

I spent most of my career reading documents that had to be right.

A requirements specification for an aircraft does not get the benefit of the doubt. Neither does a flight-readiness review on a satellite that costs more than a small town. When you have signed your name to whether a thing will fly, you learn to read carefully — to ask what each line actually claims, to trace every claim back to where it came from, and to say "I don't know" out loud when you don't, because the alternative gets people hurt.

About ten years ago, mostly at a kitchen table after the workday was done, I started reading the first chapter of Genesis the same way I read everything else that crosses my desk: as an engineer reading a specification. Not as a sermon. Not as a poem. As a description of a system, written down, with an order to it and an architecture to it. I wanted to know what that system would look like if you actually built it.

What I found is the reason this book exists. The architecture in Genesis 1 — the waters, the firmament, the boundary, the order of the days — lines up, again and again, with the architecture modern physics has independently mapped. Not loosely. Not by squinting. The matches are specific enough that I have spent a decade testing them, trying to break them, and writing down the places where I still can't. This is the family's-table version of that work.

**Who this book is for.** It is for the Christian family that does not want to choose. For the homeschool parent who loves the Bible, takes science seriously, and is tired of being told those two loves are at war. For the mom who is strong in faith and was never strong in science, and who has a fourteen-year-old about to ask a hard question. You do not need a science degree to teach from this book. That was a design requirement, not an afterthought. If a parent without a science background can't follow a chapter, the chapter is wrong, and I rewrote it.

**The promise.** Scripture comes first in every chapter. We read what the text says, in its own words and often in its original Hebrew, before we say one word about physics. Then — and only then — we look at what physics has found, and we let it confirm or fail to confirm. The Bible is never bent to fit the science, and the science is never bent to fit the Bible. Scripture leads. Physics follows. Where they meet, we'll stand on it. Where they don't yet, I'll tell you that too.

**The ethic.** I will not overclaim. The faith-and-science shelf is crowded with people who are certain about things they have not earned the right to be certain about, on both sides. I was trained, in a community where overclaiming had real consequences, to tell you exactly what I know, nothing more, and to flag the difference between what I'm confident of, what I suspect, and what is still an open question. You will see those labels throughout this book. I have built things that failed. I have run calculations that were wrong. Admitting that is not weakness; it is the only thing that makes the rest of it trustworthy.

I am not a physicist by training, and I'm not a theologian. I'm an operator who reads carefully and answers *why.* What follows is my engineering report on the first page of the Bible — written for your kitchen table, the way it was first worked out at mine.

— Jeff L. Raymond
