# Appendix D: Experiments You Can Do at Home

The physics in this book isn't locked behind laboratory equipment or college budgets. Every concept here has a demonstration you can do in your kitchen, garage, or backyard using materials you already own or can buy for a few dollars.

These demonstrations serve one purpose: **to make the invisible visible**. They are not meant to "prove" the Bible or "prove" science. They are meant to let you *see* what the chapter was describing in words. When your daughter watches salt dance on a vibrating speaker, she is not watching a proof—she is watching the same principle that holds the firmament in place. When you drop a feather in a vacuum, you are not validating Einstein—you are seeing for yourself what the chapter claimed about gravity.

The best time to do these is *while you are reading the chapter*, or immediately after. The conversation that happens *during* the experiment is where the learning lands.

## By Materials You Already Have

### "Just the Bible and a Notebook"

**Genesis 1:1 in Three Translations (Ch 1)**
- Read Genesis 1:1 aloud in the King James Version, ESV, and NASB (or whatever three translations you have access to).
- What words stay the same? What changes?
- What does it tell you that all three versions agree on certain words but differ on others?
- *Why this matters:* Translation is a choice. The underlying Hebrew is precise. Careful readers notice.

**Concordance Word Search: "Waters Above" (Ch 2)**
- Using a printed concordance or online tool (BibleGateway.com has a good concordance feature), look up every verse that mentions "waters above."
- List them in order. Read each one.
- What do they have in common? What's different?
- Does "waters above" show up anywhere unexpected?
- *Why this matters:* When a concept appears throughout Scripture, it's probably important. The Bible echoes itself.

**Scripture Search: God as Light (Ch 3)**
- Read your way through 1 John 1:5, Psalm 27:1, Exodus 13:21, Isaiah 60:19, and John 1:4-5.
- Underline every description of light or darkness.
- Is God *like* light, or *is* light in some deeper way?
- *Why this matters:* This isn't poetry; it's a claim about God's nature. Light in Genesis 1:3 connects to light in 1 John 1:5.

**Seven-Day Creation Chart (Ch 4)**
- On a large sheet of paper, draw seven columns, one for each day.
- In the first row, write what Scripture says happened each day (Genesis 1:3-31).
- In the second row, write what physics says the universe actually shows us (refer back to Ch 4 as you work).
- In the third row, write the pattern name the framework identifies (state differentiation, zone instantiation, matter formation, etc.).
- *Why this matters:* You're not forcing a match. You're *seeing* whether the match is there. The answer will tell you something.

**Conservation Laws Chart (Ch 8)**
- List five physical laws: conservation of energy, conservation of momentum, thermodynamics (energy runs downhill), light speed limit, gravity's constancy.
- Next to each, write a Scripture verse about God's character that matches it.
  - Energy conservation → "I am the LORD, I change not" (Mal 3:6)
  - Time-symmetry (why energy is conserved) → "Jesus Christ the same yesterday, today, and forever" (Heb 13:8)
  - And so on.
- *Why this matters:* Laws of physics aren't arbitrary rules imposed on the universe. They're reflections of God's faithfulness.

**Genesis 6–9 Physical Details List (Ch 11)**
- Read Genesis 6–9 aloud as a family (it's shorter than you think).
- As you read, list every physical detail the text gives: rain, fountains breaking up, water height, duration, categories of creatures, the ark's dimensions, etc.
- Circle the ones that sound like the author was *describing something he understood*, not just inventing a story.
- *Why this matters:* The Flood isn't presented as magic. It's presented as a catastrophic *event*—massive, yes, but describable in physical terms.

**Quantum Discussion and Reflection (Ch 13)**
- Read Hebrews 1:1-3 aloud: "And he is the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature, and he upholds the universe by the word of his power."
- What does "upholds" mean? (Sustains. Keeps going. Moment by moment.)
- Quantum mechanics shows us that reality at the smallest scale is *weird*—not determined, not hidden, but *actively sustained*. 
- What does it mean to believe that this active sustaining is God's power?
- *Why this matters:* This is not a proof of God. It's an invitation to see the mystery physicists are finding as the same mystery Scripture names.

**Family Discussion Framework (Ch 14)**
- Gather around a table with this chapter open.
- Choose one concept from chapters 1-13 that your family is curious about.
- Ask the question: "Why do you think God would make the universe work *this way*?" (rather than "Does this prove God exists?")
- Let each person answer. No "right answer." Just thinking together.
- *Why this matters:* Science and faith conversations don't happen in lectures. They happen in questions.

**Closing Family Prayer and Next Steps (Ch 15)**
- Sit together and read Revelation 21:1-5 aloud: "And I heard a loud voice from the throne saying, 'Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. He will dwell with them, and they will be his people, and God himself will be with them as their God.'"
- Pray together about what you've learned.
- Look together at the Recommended Reading list. Is there one thing a family member wants to explore next?
- *Why this matters:* The end of a book is not the end of the conversation. It's an invitation to keep going.

### "What's in the Kitchen"

**Light and Color with a Prism (Ch 3)**
- Shine a flashlight or bright lamp through a glass or transparent prism onto white paper.
- Observe the light separating into colors.
- What does this tell you about white light? (It's a mixture. Not simple. Made of components.)
- Try different angles. What changes?
- *Why this matters:* When God said "Let there be light" on Day 1, He separated light from darkness. This isn't just ON/OFF. Light has structure. Components. Physics.

**Cornstarch on a Speaker (Ch 7 – Standing Waves)**
- Pour a thin layer of cornstarch (or salt, or sand) into a shallow baking pan.
- Place the pan on top of a speaker.
- Play music through the speaker, starting quietly and gradually increasing volume.
- Watch the cornstarch jump and form patterns.
- Try different frequencies (adjust the song, or use an app that plays tones).
- *Why this matters:* The patterns forming in the cornstarch are standing waves. The same principle creates matter. The Bible says "let the waters be gathered" (Day 3). Physics says matter forms as standing waves on the firmament. You're seeing it happen.

### "What's in the Garage or Backyard"

**Stretched Sheet Gravity Demo (Ch 5 – The Firmament)**
- Drape a white or light-colored bed sheet across four chairs arranged in a rough square.
- Pull the sheet tight (but not so tight it tears).
- Place a tennis ball or marble in the center of the sheet.
- Observe: the weight of the ball curves the sheet downward. Objects near it roll toward the curve.
- This is how gravity works. The massive object (the Earth, the Sun) curves the fabric of space. Smaller objects follow the curve.
- *Why this matters:* Ezekiel describes the firmament as "stretched forth." This is what stretching looks like. And gravity is what happens when mass curves the stretch.

**Stretched Sheet with Marble Gravity Demo – Version 2 (Ch 9)**
- Same setup as above, but add several coins or small weights around the marble.
- The marble curves the sheet. The coins roll toward it.
- Now add more marbles. Do they roll toward each other? (Yes—through the curved sheet.)
- This is how the universe works. Gravity is geometry.
- *Why this matters:* This is the image that will help your child understand why gravity doesn't need a force pulling things "at a distance." It's curvature. The sheet is curved, and objects follow the curve.

**Star-Gazing Evening (Ch 12 – Cosmic Objects)**
- Choose a clear night (new moon is best; avoid city lights if possible).
- Go outside with a blanket or chairs.
- Bring binoculars (optional but helpful).
- Look for:
  - The Milky Way band (the dusty river of light across the sky)
  - Individual stars and constellations
  - Planets (Venus, Jupiter, Saturn if visible)
  - Galaxy clusters or nebulae if you can find them with binoculars
  - The Big Dipper (easy to find; a good starting point)
- Read Psalm 19:1-4 aloud together: "The heavens declare the glory of God."
- Discuss: what do you see? What does it make you feel?
- *Why this matters:* The universe is not a textbook fact. It's a presence. When you look up, you are looking at billions of years of history, vast distances, and structures that dwarf human understanding. And the Bible says these structures *declare* something. What are they declaring to you?

## By Concept Demonstrated

### Standing Waves and Pattern-Making (Day 3 Physics)

**Chladni Plate with Sand (Ch 7 – PRIMARY DEMO)**

*Equipment needed:* Metal plate (flat, rigid, roughly 12x12 inches), fine sand or salt, violin bow or electric speaker.

*If you have access to the equipment:*
1. Sprinkle sand lightly across the metal plate.
2. Use a violin bow to draw along the edges of the plate, or attach the plate to a speaker playing a tone at a specific frequency.
3. Watch the sand jump and settle into geometric patterns (circles, lines, triangles, spirals).
4. Change the frequency. The patterns change.
5. Each pattern represents a *mode of vibration*—a specific way the plate can oscillate.

*If you don't have the equipment:*
- Watch a Chladni plate video online. YouTube has excellent slow-motion recordings.
- The principle is the same: vibration creates visible patterns. Particles find the nodes (where vibration stops) and settle there.

*Why this matters:* Matter is made of stable patterns. Every atom is a standing wave in the fabric of space. You're watching the universe's architecture revealed in sand.

## How to Debrief an Experiment

The experiment itself isn't the learning. The conversation *after* the experiment is.

### The Conversation Template

**During the activity:**
- Don't explain much. Let people *see* first.
- Ask open questions: "What do you notice? What changed? What stayed the same?"
- Let kids touch, try, fail, try again.

**Right after:**
- Ask: "What did you see?" (observation)
- Then: "Why do you think that happened?" (hypothesis—no "right answer")
- Then: "How does this connect to what the chapter said?" (connection to concept)
- Then: "What does this tell you about how God built the universe?" (reflection)

**Later (at dinner, a few days later):**
- Mention it casually: "Remember when we watched the cornstarch jump? That's actually what's happening inside atoms."
- Let your kid bring it up again if they want to.
- These conversations are slow. Trust the process.

### Questions That Work Well

- "If the Bible says God 'stretched out' the firmament, what does 'stretched out' mean after you've felt what a sheet stretched across chairs feels like?"
- "When you saw the cornstarch form patterns, did it look like it was choosing the pattern, or did the pattern just happen?"
- "If gravity really is curvature—if it's the sheet bending—then is gravity a force, or is it shape?"
- "What surprised you most about what we just did?"

### What NOT to Do

- Don't use the experiment to "prove" the Bible. (The Bible doesn't need proving. It's the Word of God.)
- Don't oversimplify the connection. (A prism isn't "proof" that Genesis 1:3 is true. It's a demonstration of how light actually works.)
- Don't lecture. (Let the experiment speak. Your job is to ask questions, not to explain.)
- Don't rush. (Some kids need to try five times before they understand. That's fine.)

## A Closing Note

None of these experiments requires a PhD. None of them "proves" anything. But all of them show something true.

When your child watches sand dance on a vibrating plate, she isn't watching a *proof* of the architecture—she is watching *evidence* that the architecture is there. And that's worth the fifteen minutes of setup, the mess, and the conversation afterward.

The goal isn't to turn your kitchen into a laboratory. It's to help your family see that the universe your eyes show you matches the universe the Bible describes and the universe physics has discovered. Those three visions are one vision.

And that vision starts with looking carefully. Trying. Watching. Asking. That's what these experiments are for.
