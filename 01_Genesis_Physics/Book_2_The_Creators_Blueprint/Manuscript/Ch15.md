# Chapter 15 — The God Who Built the Universe Loves You

> *"And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are called according to his purpose."*
> — Romans 8:28, KJV

---

## 1. A Wife's Verse, At the Kitchen Table

It is a Sunday evening. The Black Angus cattle are settled in the lower pasture. The dog is asleep on a folded canvas coat by the wood-stove. The kitchen window faces west, and the late spring light is doing the long honey-colored thing the Columbia Gorge does at this hour. My wife has her Bible open on the table in front of her. The legal pad from last week's family meeting is beside it, the one with the block letters on Deuteronomy 6.

She is not on Deuteronomy tonight. She is on Romans 8.

She reads the verse aloud. *And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are called according to his purpose.* She reads it slowly the way she read Genesis 1 the first time, ten years ago, when this whole project began at this same table.

Then she does the thing she has done from the beginning. She looks up at me. *That word,* she says. *All things. We have been talking about all things for fourteen chapters. What is Paul actually claiming, here?*

The whole book has been a conversation with that question. The closing chapter is where the conversation finally goes where it has been going the whole time.

You have read fourteen chapters of architecture. You have walked through the waters above and the waters below, the firmament that the Hebrew calls *raqia*, the light that came on Day One before there was a sun to make it, the sustaining coupling that Hebrews calls *upholding all things by the word of his power*, the Sabbath Boundary that separates the *making* of creation from the *keeping* of it. You have heard the framework's confidence labels — HIGH, MODERATE, OPEN — applied to every claim. You have been honored as a reader. The book has not asked you to take anything on a hand-wave.

This chapter does not ask you to take anything on a hand-wave either. But it asks for something the other fourteen chapters have not asked for. It asks you to step back from the handiwork and let the book name, plainly, the *Hand.*

That is the chapter. The architecture has earned the right to point to its Architect.

## 2. Fourteen Chapters, One Hand

Step back from the kitchen for a moment and look at the book as a single object.

Part I read Genesis 1 as a specification. Hebrew vocabulary you had been told for a hundred years to read as poetry — *bara, raqia, mayim, yom* — turned out to be the vocabulary an engineer uses to describe a built system. The first page of the Bible was not a fairy tale a pre-scientific people told around a fire. It was a description of a system, written in the language of someone who had built it.

Part II walked the architecture itself. The waters above are what physics today calls dark energy. The waters below are what physics today calls dark matter. The firmament is where visible matter lives. Five percent of the universe is what we see. The other ninety-five percent is in the same places Genesis 1 said the *waters* were. That alignment is not what we expected. It is what the Hebrew text said three and a half thousand years ago.

Part III walked the hardest questions modern science has produced and showed that the framework engages each one without turning the brain off. Starlight, the Flood, black holes, the cosmic web, the strangeness of quantum mechanics — none of those chapters required pretending the data was not real. The framework took the data seriously, took the Bible seriously, and let both speak.

Part IV came home to the household. Chapter 14 named the parent as the primary teacher and the kitchen table as the primary classroom. The framework is not a curriculum and not an apologetics weapon; it is a way of seeing the whole creation that returns the family to *worship.*

That is the shape of the book. Four parts, fourteen chapters, one architecture.

But for fourteen chapters the book has done something a careful reader will have noticed. It has been very specific about *what* is upheld, and it has been very specific about *how* the upholding happens — present tense, active, continuous, structurally load-bearing — and it has been very *quiet* about the One who is doing the upholding. Hebrews 1:3 in Chapter 13 named *him* in a participle: *upholding all things by the word of his power.* The book did not chase the antecedent. It let the architecture carry the weight.

That was on purpose. Reverence required it. The book did not want to start with a sermon. It wanted to start with the *thing* — and let the thing, slowly, point to the One who made it.

We have come to where it points.

## 3. The Architect Named

The Architect of the architecture is named, plainly, in three passages of the New Testament. I am going to quote them in order, the way the book has quoted every passage — in full, with the Greek where the Greek matters, and with the engineering posture the book has held all the way through.

The first passage is John 1.

> *"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. The same was in the beginning with God. All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made."*
> — John 1:1–3, KJV

Read the third verse again. *All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made.* The Greek on *Word* is *Logos.* The word the Greeks used for *reason, structure, the rational principle that organizes the cosmos.* John takes that word — the one a Greek philosopher would have recognized instantly — and identifies the Logos with a *Person.* Eleven verses later, John names Him plainly:

> *"And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father,) full of grace and truth."*
> — John 1:14, KJV

That Person is Jesus. The same *Logos* through whom all things were made took on flesh, walked through Galilee, and dwelt among His own creatures. The Architect entered the architecture. That sentence is the New Testament's central claim, and the rest of the New Testament works it out.

The second passage is Colossians 1. We met the first half of it in Chapter 13, when we were talking about the *cohering* of the universe — *by him all things consist.* Here is the whole paragraph.

> *"Who is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of every creature: for by him were all things created, that are in heaven, and that are in earth, visible and invisible, whether they be thrones, or dominions, or principalities, or powers: all things were created by him, and for him: and he is before all things, and by him all things consist. And he is the head of the body, the church: who is the beginning, the firstborn from the dead; that in all things he might have the preeminence. For it pleased the Father that in him should all fulness dwell; and, having made peace through the blood of his cross, by him to reconcile all things unto himself; by him, I say, whether they be things in earth, or things in heaven."*
> — Colossians 1:15–20, KJV

Chapter 13 walked verses 15–17. The architecture *consists* by Him. This chapter walks verses 18–20. The same Person who *holds the architecture together* is also the *firstborn from the dead*, who *made peace through the blood of his cross*, by whom the Father *reconciles all things to himself.* Paul does not put the creation paragraph and the redemption paragraph in two letters. He puts them in one breath. He does that because, in Paul's reading, *they are the same act, by the same Person.*

The third passage is the one we partly walked in Chapter 13. Read it now in full.

> *"God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets, hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son, whom he hath appointed heir of all things, by whom also he made the worlds; who being the brightness of his glory, and the express image of his person, and upholding all things by the word of his power, when he had by himself purged our sins, sat down on the right hand of the Majesty on high."*
> — Hebrews 1:1–3, KJV

Chapter 13 walked the *upholding all things by the word of his power.* That phrase, in the Greek participle *pheron*, was the architectural anchor of the chapter on quantum mechanics. Now read the rest of the verse. *When he had by himself purged our sins, sat down on the right hand of the Majesty on high.*

The same Greek author, in the same single sentence, names two things about the same Person. The Person *upholds all things* — present-tense, continuous, the architecture being borne moment by moment. And the Person *purged our sins by himself* — past tense, a one-time, completed act, the *aorist* the Greek uses for things that happened once and stay done. Upholding the architecture, and dying for the sins of the world, are *not two unrelated specialties of two different beings.* They are the work of one Person.

The Person is Jesus Christ.

I am going to be careful here, because I have been careful all the way through this book. *I cannot prove this to you from physics.* The framework I have walked in fourteen chapters is consistent with what the Bible says about Christ, and the architectural language of John 1, Colossians 1, and Hebrews 1 lines up structurally with the architectural language of Genesis 1. But the *identification* of the Logos with Jesus of Nazareth, the *claim* that the One who upholds the architecture is the same One who walked through Galilee and went to the Cross and rose on the third day — that is *Christian theology*, and it rests on the witness of the New Testament writers who knew Him, the church that has confessed Him for two thousand years, and the work of the Holy Spirit in the human heart. The framework is not a substitute for any of that. The framework lets you see the architecture. *Scripture lets you see the Architect.*

I am naming Him now because the book would be dishonest if it did not. We have looked at the building for fourteen chapters. The Builder is named Jesus Christ.

## 4. Two Specifications, One Architect

Now lay the architecture and the redemption arc side by side. Look at what shows up.

In Chapter 5, the framework's first physical claim was *light came first.* Genesis 1:3 — *And God said, Let there be light: and there was light.* John 1 says the Word is the light, the *true Light, which lighteth every man that cometh into the world.* The framework's first physical claim and the New Testament's first claim about Christ are made with the same word, applied to the same Person.

In Chapter 6, we walked the architecture in one picture: waters above, firmament, waters below. The whole-Bible picture is the same shape. *Heaven above, the earth in the middle, the waters of judgment held back at the boundary.* Across thirty-five hundred years of biblical writing, that three-zone architecture stays.

In Chapter 13, we walked the *upholding* and the *cohering* — the present-tense, active load-bearing of the universe, named in Hebrews 1:3 and Colossians 1:17. The *redemption* paragraphs of those same chapters — Hebrews 1:3b, Colossians 1:18–20 — apply the same active verbs to the redeeming work. The Architect who *upholds* the cosmos *upholds* his church. The verbs are the same.

In Chapter 11, we walked the named perturbations — the Fall, the Flood, the Cross, the Consummation. Of those four named perturbations, *the Cross is the only one in the redemption column.* The framework treats the Cross as a structural-scale event: the moment the Architect Himself entered the architecture and bore, on a Roman cross, the weight that the architecture's broken Sabbath-Boundary had stored up against humanity since Eden.

In Chapter 14, we walked the household as the primary classroom. The redemption arc names the *household* the same way: *as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD,* Joshua 24:15. The kitchen table is not just where physics gets explained to the children. It is where the whole arc — creation, fall, cross, new creation — gets re-read every week.

The same disciplined hand wrote both columns.

## 5. Romans 8:28 in the Architecture's Light

Now read what my wife read at the kitchen table.

> *"And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are called according to his purpose."*
> — Romans 8:28, KJV

Read it the way you have learned to read scripture across the fourteen chapters of this book. *Slowly. With the Greek where the Greek matters.*

The phrase *all things* in the Greek is *panta*. That is the same word that runs through Colossians 1:16–17 (*all things were created by him*; *by him all things consist*), through Hebrews 1:3 (*upholding all things*), through John 1:3 (*all things were made by him*). The same word. *Panta.* Not *most things*; not *the spiritual things*; not *the things you can see at the time*. *All things.*

The verb *work together* in the Greek is *synergei.* The root is *synergos*, which gives us the English word *synergy*, but its plain Greek meaning is *co-laborers, things working as one.* Paul is naming a *coordinated activity*. Not a coincidence; not a happy accident. A *worked-together-ness.*

Hold those two against the engineering register the book has held. In Model-Based Systems Engineering, every requirement traces to its origin. You can walk the trace from a constraint at the leaf level all the way back to the source spec. *Romans 8:28 is the trace at the level of a believer's whole life.* Every event — the joy, the sorrow, the success, the loss, the morning the legal pad fills up, the night the diagnosis lands, the day a child comes home, the day a child does not — every event is being *traced together* by the Architect, toward a *good* He has specified.

I want to be careful, because the verse has been weaponized in unhelpful ways in the church. It does not say every event *feels* good. It does not say every event *is* good in isolation. It says every event is being *worked together* — co-laboring — for a good that the Architect is bringing about, in *those who love God.* The good is at the level of the whole arc, not at the level of the moment.

If you are reading this in a season of suffering, you are not being told that the suffering is good. You are being told that the same Person who is bearing the load of the architecture is bearing the load of *your* life — and is co-laboring every event toward a good He has specified, the way an MBSE-disciplined program manager traces every requirement to its source. *He is not absent from your spec. He is the Author of it.*

That is the *all things* my wife handed me at the kitchen table. The book has spent fourteen chapters showing how *panta* lives in physics. Romans 8:28 says the same *panta* lives in the life of the believer.

## 6. The Word for "Workmanship" Is Poiema

Here is the verse the chapter would not be honest without quoting. It is for the unbelieving reader who is reading along with the family — the parent, the teen, the grandparent — and it is for the believing reader who has forgotten the warmth of it. I want both readers to hear it without anyone in the room feeling cornered.

> *"For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: not of works, lest any man should boast. For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath before ordained that we should walk in them."*
> — Ephesians 2:8–10, KJV

The word *workmanship* in verse 10 is the Greek *poiema.* It is the root that gives us the English word *poem,* but in Greek it is used for *anything carefully made.* A built thing. A crafted thing. A piece of work whose maker can be named.

Paul is taking the engineering register the book has held — the register of a *built system, by a Maker who can be named* — and applying it to *the redeemed person.* You, in Christ, are *poiema.* Carefully made. Crafted. Worked on by the same hand that wrote the engineering spec of Genesis 1.

Read verses 8 and 9 again. *By grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God.* The Greek on *gift* is *dōron* — a present given without expectation of return. Salvation, in the New Testament, is not a thing you earn by being good enough at physics or theology or church attendance. It is a *gift the Architect offers* — by grace, through faith — to anyone who will receive it.

I want to say this plainly enough that an unbelieving teenager reading along with his Christian parents tonight can hear it cleanly, and pastorally enough that no one in the room feels pressured.

*The Architect of the architecture you have just walked through fourteen chapters of is offering to make you* poiema. *Not because you have earned it. Not because you have figured out the physics. As a gift, by grace, through faith in Jesus Christ, who upheld the cross the way he upholds the architecture — by bearing the weight Himself.*

That is the offer. I am not going to push it on you. The framework has held the cleared-community posture all the way through this book — *I tell you what I know, and I tell you what I don't, and I let you decide.* The same posture applies here. *I cannot prove the offer to you with physics.* I have shown you the architecture. I have named the Architect. I have shown you that the same disciplined hand wrote both columns of the spec. *The offer is yours to take or to set down. The Architect is patient. He has been patient with me.*

If you are already a believer, this verse is not new to you. But read it again with the architecture in your head. *You are poiema.* You are not a problem the Architect is putting up with. You are a *carefully made thing* He is still working on, and the *good works* He has ordained for you to walk in are part of the same spec that organized the cosmos.

If you are not yet a believer, the offer is open. The next-step resources at the end of this chapter will tell you where to walk if you would like to walk further.

That is the *Gospel-audible* movement. The chapter has named it. We move on.

## 7. Behold, I Make All Things New

The Bible opens with a specification of a built system.

The Bible *closes* with the promise of a new specification — a new heaven and a new earth, the architecture remade. Read it now, slowly, in full.

> *"And I saw a new heaven and a new earth: for the first heaven and the first earth were passed away; and there was no more sea. And I John saw the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down from God out of heaven, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband. And I heard a great voice out of heaven saying, Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them, and they shall be his people, and God himself shall be with them, and be their God. And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away. And he that sat upon the throne said, Behold, I make all things new. And he said unto me, Write: for these words are true and faithful."*
> — Revelation 21:1–5, KJV

The architecture is not abandoned. It is *remade.* That distinction matters, and the chapter does not want to slide past it. A Maker who loved His creation enough to *make* it does not, at the end, *throw it away.* He *finishes* it. The same hand that drew the first line of the spec on the first day draws the last line of the same spec at the consummation. The architecture you have walked for fourteen chapters is not a temporary scaffold to be discarded; it is the rough-build of the dwelling that will be complete when the Architect dwells in it openly.

*A new heaven and a new earth.* The same architecture you have walked for fourteen chapters — the heavens above, the earth in the middle — but new. Not different in *kind.* Different in *condition.* The Greek on *new* is *kainos*, which is the word for *fresh, restored, made-new* rather than *brand-new-from-scratch.* The architecture the Architect built does not get scrapped. It gets *brought to its finished state.*

*And there was no more sea.* The Hebrew imagination's image for the chaotic, unbroken waters of Genesis 1:2 — *the deep* over which the Spirit hovered before the architecture was spoken — is *resolved.* In the new creation, the waters are fully ordered. The architectural separation that Day One began is *complete.*

*Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them.* The sustaining coupling — the present-tense, active *upholding* the framework names in Chapter 13 — is at *full strength.* No veil. No Sabbath Boundary anymore. The Architect dwells with His creatures, the way He walked with Adam and Eve in the cool of the day in Genesis 3:8 before the Fall — but now without the Fall having happened. The architecture's design intent, finally realized.

*And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain.* The named perturbations of Chapter 11 — the Fall, the Flood, the Cross — were necessary acts in a fallen architecture. In the new creation, they are *not necessary.* The architecture has been brought to its sustaining-mode finish. The thermodynamic damage of the Fall is undone.

*Behold, I make all things new.* Read the verb. *Make.* Present tense. The same active load-bearing the book has been describing for fourteen chapters — *upholding*, *cohering*, *bearing the load* — now in its consummation phase. The Architect is not future-tense in this verse. He is *making.* Right now. *All things.* *Panta* one final time.

The framework's *all things* and the Bible's *all things* are the same *all things.* The Architect's first sentence and the Architect's last sentence are spoken by the same Voice.

I have read this passage at funerals. I have read it at weddings. I read it now, at the close of this book, because the architecture would not be complete without it. Genesis 1 and Revelation 21 are the two ends of one arc, and the arc has held since before the foundation of the world.

## 8. A New Creation, Already

The new heaven and new earth are coming. We are honest, in the framework's vocabulary, that the *not yet* is real — the architecture in its current condition still bears the named perturbations of Chapter 11. There is still a Sabbath Boundary. There are still tears.

But the new-creation work has *already begun.*

> *"Therefore if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new."*
> — 2 Corinthians 5:17, KJV

The Greek on *new creature* is *kainē ktisis* — a *fresh-made-creation.* The same root as the verb *bara* in Genesis 1:1, run through the Greek lens of the New Testament. Paul uses the same architectural word for cosmic creation — *ktisis* — for what God does to the believer. *The Architect of cosmos is the Architect of you.* And the *making-new* He has begun in His people is part of the same arc that ends in Revelation 21.

That has consequences for the kitchen table on Sunday evening.

The Christian household at Sunday-evening reading is not waiting passively for the new creation to arrive. The household *is, in a small way, already a piece of it.* The way the wood-stove in our shop warms the room before the world outside has thawed — first heat radiates from a small source; the warming spreads — a household where Christ is honored is a small new-creation, warming the room before the world outside the door is warm. The framework would call that an *open-system input* in a fallen *closed-system* environment. The theology calls it *salt and light* (Matthew 5:13–14). It is the same architecture, named in two registers.

That is why Chapter 14 was right to name the parent as the primary teacher and the kitchen table as the primary classroom. The kitchen table is *participation in the new creation already.*

The wood-stove eventually goes out. The *making-new* the Architect is doing in His people does not.

## 9. What Manner of Love

The chapter title is *The God Who Built the Universe Loves You.*

The architecture, by itself, would only show *power.* A Maker who could specify the speed of light, hammer out the firmament, hold the waters above and the waters below in their bounded reservoirs, and uphold the whole thing moment by moment for thirteen and a half billion years — that is power.

Romans 8:28, the Cross, the new creation — these show *love.*

> *"Behold, what manner of love the Father hath bestowed upon us, that we should be called the sons of God: therefore the world knoweth us not, because it knew him not."*
> — 1 John 3:1, KJV

Read the first phrase. *What manner of love.* The Greek is *potapēn* — *what kind of, of what foreign country, from what far-away place.* John uses the word in *surprise.* The love that has been bestowed on the believers is of a *kind* the world had not seen before God revealed it. It is foreign to the natural order, the way an open-system input is foreign to a closed-system equation.

I am not going to try to *prove* this to you from physics. The book has been honest all the way through about what physics can and cannot show. Physics can show you the architecture. Physics cannot show you the *love* of the Architect. That is not a failure of physics. It is a feature of the *kind* of thing love is — relational, personal, given, received.

What the framework *can* tell you is this: the same Person who specified the architecture, who upholds it moment by moment, who bore the weight of the Cross, who is making all things new — *that Person* is the One the New Testament names *Father.* The love is *His.* The relationship is *offered.* The reader who would like to step into it is welcome. The reader who is already in it can rest in it more deeply tonight than they did before they read this chapter.

*Behold, what manner of love.*

You take that on the same kind of trust on which a child takes a parent's love — not by proof, but by relationship. The framework cannot tell you the relationship is real. The framework can tell you that it is *consistent with everything else in the architecture* and that the architecture has held up under fourteen chapters of testing.

The rest is between you and the Architect.

## 10. Doxology

Paul ends the longest theological argument in the New Testament — the eleven chapters of Romans 1 through 11, in which he walks the redemption arc from the universal need of humanity through the work of Christ — with a doxology. Not with another argument. With *praise.* The chapter must end where Paul ended.

> *"O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God! how unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out! For who hath known the mind of the Lord? or who hath been his counsellor? Or who hath first given to him, and it shall be recompensed unto him again? For of him, and through him, and to him, are all things: to whom be glory for ever. Amen."*
> — Romans 11:33–36, KJV

Read it slowly. Read it twice.

The Greek on *unsearchable* is *anexeraunētos* — *not-traceable-out.* It is the word that names the limit of MBSE itself. There is a level at which the trace ends in *worship*, not in another spec document. The architecture's design margin runs all the way back to the Designer Himself, and at some point the engineer puts down the pencil and says *amen.* I have learned this slowly over a lot of years. The serious engineers I have known — at the National Reconnaissance Office, at Insitu, at OKSI — all reach a point where they sit back from the whiteboard and say, *that's as far as the tools take us.* Romans 11:33 names that point in the believer's vocabulary.

*For of him, and through him, and to him, are all things.* That is the same *panta* one final time. *Of him* — the Architect is the source. *Through him* — the Architect is the agent. *To him* — the Architect is the destination. The framework has spent fourteen chapters on *of him* and *through him.* The destination is *to him.* Worship is not an ornament on the framework. *Worship is what the framework was built to point at.*

*To whom be glory for ever. Amen.*

That is the chapter's scripture memory verse. Tape it inside the front cover of your Bible. Read it at the close of every family meeting for the rest of the year. The book has done its argument. What remains is praise.

## Key Terms

- **Logos (λόγος)** — Greek for *word, reason, the rational principle that organizes the cosmos.* John 1:1 identifies the Logos with the Person of Jesus Christ.
- **Poiema (ποίημα)** — Greek for *workmanship, anything carefully made.* The root of the English word *poem.* Paul applies it to the redeemed person in Ephesians 2:10 — the engineering register of the Family Edition turned toward the soul.
- **Panta (πάντα)** — Greek for *all things.* The same word runs through Colossians 1:16–17, Hebrews 1:3, John 1:3, Romans 8:28, and Romans 11:36. The framework's *all things* and Paul's *all things* are the same *all things.*
- **Synergei (συνεργεῖ)** — Greek verb in Romans 8:28, *work together.* From the root *synergos*, *co-laborers.* Names a *coordinated activity*, not a coincidence.
- **Kainē ktisis (καινὴ κτίσις)** — Greek for *new creation, fresh-made creation.* 2 Corinthians 5:17. The same architectural word for cosmic creation, applied to the regenerate believer.
- **Kainos (καινός)** — Greek for *new* in the sense of *fresh, restored*, rather than *brand-new-from-scratch.* The architecture of Revelation 21 is *kainos* — the same architecture, brought to its finished state.
- **Anexeraunētos (ἀνεξεραύνητος)** — Greek for *unsearchable, not-traceable-out.* Romans 11:33. Names the limit of MBSE itself — the point at which the trace ends in worship, not in another spec.
- **Potapēn (ποταπήν)** — Greek for *what kind of, of what foreign country.* 1 John 3:1. John uses the word in surprise — the Father's love is of a *kind* the world had not seen before God revealed it.
- **Soli Deo gloria** — Latin, *to God alone be the glory.* The closing posture of the Reformation tradition. The chapter, and the book, end with it.

## Family Discussion Questions

1. *(Elementary, ages 5–10.)* The chapter says God *upholds* the whole world all the time — like someone holding a heavy thing on his shoulder, but never getting tired. What is something heavy that you have tried to hold? Did your arms get tired? How is God's holding different?
2. *(Elementary, ages 5–10.)* Read Revelation 21:5 aloud together: *Behold, I make all things new.* What is one thing in your life you would like God to make new? What is one thing in the world you would like God to make new?
3. *(Middle school, ages 11–14.)* The chapter says the same Greek word — *all things*, *panta* — runs through Colossians 1, Hebrews 1, John 1, Romans 8, and Romans 11. Read those verses together as a family. What is the *one* thing the Bible is consistently saying about *all things*? Why might that consistency matter?
4. *(Middle school, ages 11–14.)* The author says, "*I cannot prove the offer to you with physics.*" What is the difference between *proving* something with physics, and *receiving* something as a gift? Can you give an example from your own life of something important that physics could not prove, but that you believe is real?
5. *(High school, ages 14+.)* Read Ephesians 2:8–10 again. The Greek for *workmanship* in verse 10 is *poiema* — *carefully made thing.* If a person is *poiema*, what kind of relationship does that person have with the Maker? How is that different from being a *random product* of an impersonal process?
6. *(High school, ages 14+.)* Romans 8:28 says *all things work together for good* — but it does *not* say all things *feel* good in the moment. Have you ever lived through something that did not feel good at the time, that later you saw God use for good? Are you living through something now that you cannot yet see the *good* of? How does the verse hold up when you cannot see the trace?
7. *(Parents.)* The chapter ends with the words *Soli Deo gloria* — *to God alone be the glory.* What does it look like, in *your* household, this week, to live out *to God alone be the glory*? What is one habit, one conversation, or one decision that would change if those four words were the household's posture?

## Family Activity — A Closing Family Prayer

This is the closing family activity for the whole Family Edition. You have walked fifteen chapters with this book. The book ends here. The conversation continues — at your kitchen table, in your truck cab on the way to the feed store, in your bedroom doorframe at nine-thirty at night — for the rest of your life.

Tonight, close the book together with this prayer. The parent leads. Each member of the family is invited to add one line. There is no wrong line.

> *Lord God,*
> *we thank You for the architecture of creation —*
> *the light You spoke on Day One,*
> *the waters above and the waters below,*
> *the firmament that holds the heavens,*
> *and the laws You wrote into every part.*
>
> *We thank You that You are still upholding all things —*
> *this house, this table, this conversation, the people in this room.*
>
> *We thank You that You are not only the Architect,*
> *but the One who came down into the architecture —*
> *who took on flesh in Jesus Christ,*
> *who bore on the Cross the weight we could not bear,*
> *who rose on the third day,*
> *and who is, this very moment, making all things new.*
>
> *Help our household to be a small new-creation,*
> *warming the room before the world is warm.*
> *Help us to teach our children diligently — to whet the blade.*
> *Help us to read the Bible together, with humility, with hunger, with joy.*
> *Help us to worship You — for of You, and through You, and to You, are all things.*
>
> *(Each member of the family adds one line.)*
>
> *In the name of Jesus Christ our Lord,*
> *Amen.*

After the prayer, sit together at the table for a few minutes without speaking. Let the prayer settle. Then close your Bibles. Hug your children. Thank your spouse. The book is finished. The work has only begun.

## For Further Study

The book is finished, but the road is not. Two paths stand open. You can walk either. You can walk both. You can walk neither, and the book does not consider that a failure.

### For more *physics* — go deeper into the architecture

- ***Genesis Physics: The Hidden Architecture — A Physics of the First Page*** *(Book 1 of the franchise.)* The popular-science companion to this book. No scripture; no family-edition register. The same architecture, in the voice of Brian Cox or Carlo Rovelli — for the spouse, the friend, the colleague, or the older teen who wants the framework without the family-table setting. Useful for sharing the framework with someone who is not yet ready for the Bible-first register.
- ***Genesis Physics: The Foundations of Genesis Physics*** *(Book 0, six volumes.)* The graduate-level encyclopedia. Full mathematical derivations of every claim the Family Edition has rested on. For the homeschool student going to college in physics, the parent with engineering training, or the curious reader who wants the equations under the architecture. *Foundations Volume 6, Chapter 14 (Open Problems)* is the honest catalog of what the framework has not yet closed; *Foundations Volume 6, Chapter 17 (The Research Program)* is the long road forward.

### For more *Christ* — go deeper into the relationship

- **Your Bible.** Read Genesis 1 aloud as a family this week. Read John 1 the week after. Read Colossians the week after that. The Bible is the primary teacher; everything in the franchise is downstream of it.
- **Your local church.** No book — and no franchise — is a substitute for the gathered church. The household is the small new-creation; the church is the larger one. Find a Bible-believing congregation; sit under preaching that takes scripture seriously; commit to a community.
- **Prayer.** Pray together as a family — at meals, at the close of family meetings, at the bedside of a sick child, on the way out the door in the morning. The framework cannot give you the relationship. Prayer is the relationship's open door.

### Recommended teachers — for parents and older teens

No single human teacher carries the whole of the Christian faith. The local church and the Bible itself are the primary teachers. But the following authors and pastors have, in the writer's reading, served the church well across denominational lines and can be trusted for thoughtful, scripturally serious instruction. *Read with a Berean discipline (Acts 17:11) — check what they say against scripture.*

- **John Lennox** — Oxford mathematician on faith and science. *God's Undertaker; Cosmic Chemistry.* The closest comparable voice in the franchise's space.
- **C. S. Lewis** — *Mere Christianity; The Great Divorce; The Weight of Glory.* Twentieth-century classics; written for the thoughtful skeptic and the thoughtful believer alike.
- **J. I. Packer** — *Knowing God.* On who God is, in plain reverent prose.
- **Tim Keller** *(d. 2023)* — *The Reason for God; The Prodigal God.* For the urban, intellectually-restless reader and the parent of the teen leaving the faith.
- **N. T. Wright** — *Surprised by Hope; Simply Christian.* On the resurrection, the new creation, and what scripture is doing as a single arc.
- **Billy Graham** *(d. 2018)* — *Steps to Peace with God* (free, available widely from the Billy Graham Evangelistic Association). The simplest, plainest next-step booklet for the unbelieving reader who has heard the Gospel in this chapter and would like to take the next step.

For the homeschool student: read your Bible first; the listed authors second; this book as a companion to both, never as a substitute for either.
