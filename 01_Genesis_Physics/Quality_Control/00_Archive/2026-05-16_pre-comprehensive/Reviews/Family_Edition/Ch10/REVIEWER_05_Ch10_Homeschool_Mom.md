## What I Loved

**"The road was growing under the light."**

Okay. That sentence. That was the moment I stopped taking notes and just... sat with it. Because the night my 16-year-old asks me — and she *will* ask me — "How can we see light from galaxies billions of light-years away if the universe is only six thousand years old?" I now have a sentence I can actually say back that makes sense and doesn't feel like I'm hedging or hiding.

The ladybug-on-the-balloon analogy is *gold*. My 14-year-old has a concrete, memorable picture now. Light walks at a constant speed. The space underneath it stretches. The distance gets covered anyway. Done. It works.

But here's what really got me: the opening scene works. Jeff and his wife's cousin on the back porch at night, looking up at Andromeda. That's *real*. My kids know what that feels like — I've taken them out under the stars enough times. The question isn't academic. It's the question you actually ask when you're standing under the sky and somebody says "that thing is 2.5 million light-years away." The chapter starts there, not in equations or abstract cosmology. It starts with a person asking a real question, and an engineer taking a long breath and actually answering it.

The Hebrew work is beautifully done. I now understand that *raqia* doesn't mean a hard dome — it means a beaten-out, stretched thing. The verb *raqa* used in Exodus 39:3 (gold beaten thin) and Numbers 16:39 (bronze censers) makes it concrete. My hands have never worked gold or bronze, but I've seen it done. That helped. And the fact that Isaiah uses "stretching" language *five times in six chapters* — that made me realize this isn't a fringe argument the author invented. It's right there in the text, repeated and insistent.

**The disavowal of "light in transit" is necessary and done right.** The chapter says plainly: if God made photons that never corresponded to real events (supernovas that never happened, collisions that never happened), that's fabricated history. And Numbers 23:19 — *God is not a man that he should lie* — closes that door. I can teach that. My 16-year-old won't be able to argue with it because it's not dodging the problem; it's taking it seriously and saying "no, that answer makes God a liar, and the Bible won't let us say that."

**The section on "Three Honest Readings" (section 8) is exactly what I needed.** My sister-in-law is a geology teacher and an old-Earth creationist. My brother reads the Days as long ages. I've felt a little defensive about teaching my kids the young-Earth reading because I didn't want them to think people who disagree are heretics. This chapter gives me language: "These three readings have different implications for starlight and time... all three take the text seriously. All three take the science seriously. All three confess Christ. All three have a place at the family table." That's not mushy. That's honest. And it models for my kids how to disagree with family and still love them.

**The confidence levels (section 9) — RIGOROUS / WORKING / OPEN — is exactly the tone I needed to hear.** The chapter says: the Hebrew evidence for stretching is rock-solid. The two-verb grammar is rock-solid. The physics of light in an expanding space is textbook. But the exact magnitude of the creation-week expansion rate? That's a working model. The detailed CMB interpretation? Still being tested. I can say that to my kids. "We know this. We're still working on that. We don't know yet." That's not weakness. That's credibility. A parent without a science background gets permission to say "I know what I know and I'm honest about what I don't."

**The family activity is doable and smart.** Find five scientists (Christian or not) across three positions and read why they hold their view. That's a research project my kids can actually do. It's not make-a-model busy-work. It's real intellectual work. My 16-year-old will *want* to do this because it's not the activity a sermon would assign; it's what a thinking person would do when faced with disagreement. And the suggested names — Russell Humphreys, Francis Collins, Hugh Ross, John Lennox — are real people with real credentials. Not a straw man. Not a caricature.

## Can I Teach This?

**Absolutely yes.**

I can stand in front of my kids and say: "The Hebrew word for the sky is *raqia*, which comes from a verb meaning *to beat out thin and stretch*. Isaiah uses stretching language five times in six chapters. Modern cosmology says the universe is expanding. So what if the expansion was *fast* in the beginning and *slow* now? During the fast part, light from newly-made stars could travel across a much shorter distance. The speed of light didn't change. The space between things grew. That's it."

I can do that because Jeff doesn't use equations. He uses pictures. The ladybug. The raisin bread. The porch at night. All of those are things my family has *done* or *seen*. I don't have to pretend to understand things I don't. I just have to understand the analogy.

And I can answer the follow-ups:
- "But what about the 13.8 billion years?" — That assumes the rate stayed constant. Scripture and modern theory both suggest it didn't.
- "What about radiometric dating?" — Next chapter. Same family of answer. Different mechanism.
- "What about Christians who disagree?" — They're our family too. We don't all have to land in the same place.

I can say those things and not feel like I'm hedging.

## Discussion Questions

The chapter provides six discussion questions at the end:

1. *Before reading this chapter, how would you have answered...? Has your answer changed?* — Real. Reflection-based. Not yes/no.
2. *Put the Sabbath Boundary in your own words.* — Requires understanding, not recall.
3. *Read Isaiah 45:12, 40:22, and Psalm 147:4 out loud together. How do they line up with what modern telescopes show us?* — Connects text to observation. Not just "what does the verse mean" but "what does the verse mean *in light of what we can see*?"
4. *Why does the chapter reject the light-in-transit answer? What would it mean about God's character?* — Theological depth. Not just mechanics.
5. *Which reading does your family hold? Do you know people who hold one of the others? Can you still treat them as family?* — Personal and relational. Will actually start a dinner-table conversation.
6. *Memorize Isaiah 40:26. Then go outside on a clear night and say it out loud, looking up. How does that feel different from reading it on the page?* — This is an *assignment* that moves the reader outside. Into the actual night sky. Looking at the actual stars. That's not an activity you can fail at. It's an invitation.

These are not quiz questions. These are *conversation starters*. My family would use these.

## "But What About?" Section

The chapter has seven "but what about" objections and answers:

1. *The 13.8 billion years number in my child's textbook* — Answered. The number comes from extrapolating one rate backward. Scripture names a different rate for creation-week. Take the right rate, get a different number.

2. *Radiometric dating also gives billions of years* — Deferred to Ch11 (appropriate). Short answer given. Not dodged.

3. *Christians who say the days were long ages* — Honored as brothers and sisters. Not called heretics. This is the section that prepares me for my sister-in-law's house at Thanksgiving.

4. *The speed of light used to be faster* — Named and disavowed. The *c-decay* hypothesis. Explained why it's wrong (contradicts Ch8's constancy claim). Contrasted with the framework's actual claim (fabric stretched, not *c* changed).

5. *The cosmic microwave background* — This one delighted me. Instead of saying "we don't know," the chapter says "our framework reads it as the membrane's vibrational signature, which explains its uniformity across the sky (a puzzle for standard Big Bang cosmology)." Not dodging. Offering a coherent alternative reading.

6. *James Webb telescope images of mature galaxies at the edge of the visible universe* — Called "a gift." The JWST has found galaxies too old for standard cosmology. In a framework where galaxies were made mature and functional from Day 4, this is exactly what we'd expect. A prediction confirmed. This is *gold* for a homeschool parent because your kid comes home excited about JWST and you can say "yes, *and* here's what Genesis predicted about this."

7. *What about Christians who disagree?* — Already covered in section 8. Affirmed. Not heretics.

I came into this chapter worried about the "but what about" section being evasive. It's not. It's direct. It answers the question kids actually ask. It doesn't pretend some objections don't exist. It faces them.

"It is eleven at night in late September, and my wife's cousin is sitting in the chair on my right."

Not: "The starlight paradox presents a significant challenge to young-earth creationism."

The chapter uses short sentences for technical stuff and longer sentences for pastoral stuff. When Isaiah 40:22 gets quoted, the prose slows down. "It is he who sits above the circle of the earth… who stretches out the heavens like a curtain, and spreads them like a tent to dwell in." You can *hear* that. You can feel the breath in it.

There's no preaching. There's no "creationism wins and secular cosmology loses" framing (which the chapter explicitly warns against). The tone is: "The question had a hidden assumption in it. When you ask it rightly, honest physics gives an honest answer."

For a homeschool parent, this matters. I don't want to teach my kids to *win* arguments with their friends. I want to teach them to *think clearly* and to *love people who disagree*. This chapter does that. It's not combat. It's clarity.
