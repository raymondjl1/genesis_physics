2. Radioactive Decay Physics
2.1 The Decay Law
Radioactive decay follows first-order kinetics:
dN_P/dt = -λN_P
Integration yields N_P(t) = N_P(0)exp(-λt). The daughter product accumulates: N_D(t) = N_D(0) + N_P(0)[exp(λt) - 1]. Half-life relates to decay constant: t½ = ln(2)/λ. These equations are exact given the assumptions.
2.2 Critical Assumptions
Three fundamental assumptions underlie radiometric dating:
Assumption 1: Known Initial Conditions
Standard approach assumes N_D(0) = 0 (pure parent at formation) or uses isochron methods to solve for N_D(0). Our challenge: If all isotopes created simultaneously at matter formation, then N_D(0) > 0 by design. The assumption of N_D(0) = 0 is invalid for created matter.
Assumption 2: Constant Decay Rate
Standard approach assumes λ constant over all time. Laboratory measurements confirm constancy over decades. Our position: λ IS constant in sustaining-mode physics (post-Day 7). However, decay did not operate during creation mode because conservation laws were not yet activated. Constancy post-Day 7 does not imply operation before Day 7.
Assumption 3: Closed System
Assumes no isotopes added or removed except by decay. Often valid post-Day 7. However, initial creation event represents fundamental addition—both parent and daughter added simultaneously by Waters Below condensation. Standard methods cannot detect this because it occurred before the clock started.
2.3 The Age Equation
Solving for age: t = (1/λ) × ln[1 + N_D/N_P]. Standard interpretation: t = time elapsed since formation. Our interpretation: t = functional maturity index (reflects N_D/N_P ratio established at creation plus ~6,000 years of decay). The ratio provides genuine information but interpreting it as temporal age requires assumptions about initial conditions that may not hold across Sabbath Boundary.

6. Carbon-14: Direct Evidence
6.1 The C-14 Anomaly
C-14 provides strongest direct evidence for young age. Half-life t½ = 5,730 years. After 10 half-lives (57,300 years), concentration drops to 0.001×N₀. After 17 half-lives (~100,000 years), below detection limits. Therefore materials older than ~100,000 years should contain zero detectable C-14.
6.2 C-14 Found in Ancient Materials
Yet C-14 routinely detected in materials supposedly millions of years old: Coal (300 million years conventional age), diamonds (1-3 billion years), dinosaur bones (65-230 million years), natural gas. Multiple independent studies using rigorous contamination controls still detect C-14. Amounts are small but consistently above background.
RATE project analyzed 10 coal samples (conventional ages 40-320 million years) with extreme cleaning protocols. Result: All samples contained measurable C-14. C-14 dates ranged 20,000-50,000 years—vastly younger than conventional ages, proving samples cannot be millions of years old.
6.3 Standard Responses Inadequate
Standard explanations: (1) Contamination—but rigorous cleaning should eliminate; diamonds impermeable. (2) In-situ production via uranium neutrons—but calculated rates too low; diamonds have no uranium. (3) Background—but multiple labs with different instruments get concordant results. None adequately explains consistent, reproducible C-14 detection.
6.4 Young-Earth Explanation

Diamonds formed Day 3 with trace C-14. After ~6,000 years: N/N₀ = (1/2)^1.05 ≈ 0.48 (48% remaining). Still detectable. Diamond impermeability ensures any C-14 present is original.
