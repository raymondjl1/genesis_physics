An initial intuition might be to model energy extraction as a dam — let the Waters flow through a controlled opening and harvest the flow. In standard physics, these are quantum vacuum fluctuations — the zero-point energy of the electromagnetic field. 5.1 Cochlea Anatomy
The inner ear contains a spiral-shaped, fluid-filled cavity called the cochlea (from the Greek κοχλίας, “snail”). A cross-section reveals a structure that maps precisely onto the zone architecture:
Scala vestibuli (upper chamber): Filled with perilymph fluid, transmits sound pressure from the oval window. Higher kinetic energy. Pushes the basilar membrane downward.
Basilar membrane: A thin, stretched membrane running the full length of the cochlea (~35 mm in humans). Width varies from ~100 µm at the base to ~500 µm at the apex. Stiffness decreases 100-fold from base to apex, creating a frequency-to-position mapping (tonotopic organization). Thickness at the base: approximately 30 µm.
Scala tympani (lower chamber): Filled with perilymph fluid, connected to the scala vestibuli at the apex through the helicotrema. Returns pressure waves toward the round window.
Stria vascularis: A highly vascularized layer of specialized cells lining the lateral wall of the cochlear duct. It actively pumps potassium ions (K⁺) to maintain a +80 mV endocochlear potential (EP) across the basilar membrane. This is an active, ATP-consuming process. The stria vascularis is the most metabolically active tissue in the body per unit volume.
Outer hair cells (OHCs): Approximately 12,000 electromechanical cells sitting on the basilar membrane. They are motors, not sensors. OHCs change length in response to voltage changes (electromotility via the protein prestin), actively amplifying basilar membrane vibrations by ~40 dB (100× power amplification). They draw their energy from the +80 mV endocochlear potential.
Inner hair cells (IHCs): Approximately 3,500 sensory cells that convert basilar membrane vibrations into electrical nerve impulses. Stereocilia on their surface deflect with membrane motion, opening mechanotransduction ion channels. K⁺ flows in (driven by the +80 mV EP), depolarizing the cell and triggering neurotransmitter release to the auditory nerve. These are the harvesters.

7.2 Power Extraction Formula
The gross power available from a Casimir-boundary oscillation device is:
P_gross = (F/A) × A × Δx × f × N
Where:
F/A = Casimir pressure at gap size a [Pa]
A = active plate area [m²]
Δx = amplitude of boundary oscillation [m]
f = oscillation frequency [Hz]
N = number of boundary pairs in the stack
Dimensional analysis confirms: Pa × m² × m × Hz = (N/m²)(m²)(m)(1/s) = N·m/s = W. The formula is P = F × v, where v = Δx × f is the effective boundary velocity. This represents the rate at which the oscillating boundaries do work against the Casimir field.
7.3 Net Power
The net extractable power accounts for the replenishment efficiency η and the harvesting efficiency η_harvest:
P_net = P_gross × η × η_harvest
For the reference design (Section 8):
P_gross = 208.2 Pa × 25 × 10⁻⁴ m² × 10⁻⁹ m × 1.14 × 10⁹ Hz × 200
       = 208.2 × 0.0025 × 1.14 × 10⁰ × 200
       = 118.7 W
At η = 0.5 and η_harvest = 0.6 (conservative Schottky rectenna efficiency):
P_net = 118.7 × 0.5 × 0.6 = 35.6 W
7.4 Thermal Analysis
Waste heat equals gross power minus harvested power. For the reference design:
P_waste = P_gross × (1 − η_harvest) = 118.7 × 0.4 = 47.5 W
For a copper housing approximately 10 cm per side (6 faces, surface area ~0.06 m²), with combined natural convection and radiation coefficient h ≈ 15 W/(m²·K):
ΔT = P_waste / (h × A_surface) = 47.5 / (15 × 0.06) = 53°C
Case temperature at 20°C ambient: ~73°C. This is within safe operating limits for electronic devices (typically < 85°C). A small heatsink or fan would reduce this further.
7.5 Constraints and Limits
Gap size lower limit: Below ~20 nm, stiction forces (van der Waals adhesion) dominate and gaps collapse. Surface roughness of polished silicon is 0.3–1 nm RMS; at 15 nm gap this represents 5–7% of the gap, introducing significant non-uniformity. The design point of 50 nm provides safe margin.
Amplitude constraint: The oscillation amplitude must be much less than the gap to avoid contact. Design rule: Δx < 0.1a. At a = 50 nm, this gives Δx < 5 nm. The reference design uses Δx = 1 nm (2% of gap).
Frequency constraint: The harvesting stage must match the cavity frequency. Piezoelectric materials max out at ~1 MHz; the rectenna approach supports the full 1.14 GHz cavity frequency.
Thermal limit: Maximum passive dissipation ~89 W for a 10 cm copper cube. With forced air cooling, up to ~280 W. The reference design produces 47.5 W waste heat, well within passive limits.
