Introduction: Pauli's Question.

The Mystery.

The fine structure constant alpha is the dimensionless combination

[Equation — see print edition.]

It is the strength of the electromagnetic interaction, measured in units of h-bar c and e to the 2/(4 pi epsilon sub 0), and it is one of the most precisely determined numbers in science. Twelve significant figures. You can measure it in a Penning trap using the electron's anomalous magnetic moment, or through the quantum Hall effect, or from atomic recoil in cesium, and all three methods agree to better than one part in 10 to the 9. Whatever alpha is, it is not a number we are confused about experimentally.

It is a number we have been confused about theoretically for a century.

Feynman put the problem in the epigraph above. Pauli reportedly said that when he died, his first question to God would be "Why 1/137?" Eddington spent years trying to derive alpha from integer combinations and eventually wrote papers proposing alpha to the -1 = 137 exactly (it is not). Dirac tried, failed, and went home. Every serious physicist from Einstein onward has at one point or another looked at alpha to the -1 approximately equal to 137 and wondered whether it was computed somewhere or simply written into the initial conditions of the universe.

The reason the question is so sharp is that alpha is not just a coupling — it is the only coupling of low-energy electrodynamics. Fix alpha and you have fixed the strength of every photon vertex in the Standard Model at the energy scale of chemistry. The sizes of atoms, the wavelengths of spectral lines, the binding energies of molecules, the width of the 21-cm hydrogen line, the value of e to the 2 in Gaussian units, the Compton wavelength of the electron — they are all functions of alpha, h-bar, c, m sub e. And of those four, h-bar, c, and m sub e can be absorbed into the choice of units, leaving only alpha as a number that says something irreducible about the universe.
