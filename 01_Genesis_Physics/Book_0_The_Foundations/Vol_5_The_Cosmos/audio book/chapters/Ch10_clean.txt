Foundations Vol 5: The Cosmos — Part III: Cosmology.

"Chapter nine asked the cosmic microwave background a question and received an answer. Chapter ten asks a quieter question of the matter that scattered those photons one last time and then went its own way. The question is: where did it end up? Knowing the brane FLRW background of chapter eight and the matter transfer function of chapter nine, we have, in the linear regime, no remaining freedom: the answer is forced. This chapter walks the chain that forces it, and is honest about where the chain ends and the harder problem of the nonlinear universe begins."

What This Chapter Is (and Is Not).

Before I write down a single equation I want to declare the chapter's epistemic position, because it differs in an important way from the position of chapter nine, and a reader who carries the wrong expectation across the boundary between chapters will be either disappointed or fooled.

What this chapter does. It takes the brane FLRW background derived in Vol 5 chapter 8 — the four density parameters, the era structure, the function E(z) = H(z)/H sub 0 — and the matter transfer function T(k) assembled in Vol 5 chapter 9 §9.5–§9.8, and uses them as the inputs to standard linear cosmological perturbation theory. From these inputs and from the linearized fluid equations of Vol 3 chapter 5, the chapter derives the linear growth factor the expression, the matter power spectrum P(k, a), the rms mass variance sigma (M), and the Press–Schechter halo mass function dn/d M. It compares each of these to the relevant observation: the SDSS BOSS galaxy power spectrum, the Planck-inferred matter power spectrum, the X-ray cluster mass function, and the redshift-space-distortion measurements of f sigma sub 8. It then introduces the Zel'dovich approximation in its standard form to show that the framework reproduces, qualitatively, the cosmic web pattern observed in galaxy redshift surveys. Finally, in §10.10, it brings forward the Vol 1 chapter 6 weak-self-coupling argument for the Waters Below field to argue that the framework reproduces the Bullet Cluster observation qualitatively.

What this chapter does not do, and why the difference matters. Chapter nine derived the recombination redshift, the sound horizon, the angular-diameter distance, and the first acoustic peak from the framework's Omega sub i — quantities that were themselves derived in chapter 8 from the brane radius, the brane tension, and the bulk-field calculation of Vol 1 §6.7. The chain there ran from non-cosmological inputs to cosmological observables, and the Skeptic's correct objection — did you tune anything? — had a specific and defensible answer.

In this chapter, the chain is shorter and the conclusion is correspondingly more modest. The framework's prediction for the expression, in the canonical case where the Waters Above behaves as a strict cosmological constant (w sub A = -1 exactly), is numerically identical to the LambdaCDM growth factor with the same Omega sub m and Omega _ Lambda. The framework's prediction for sigma sub 8 is numerically identical to the LambdaCDM prediction with the same A sub s, n sub s, T(k). The framework's prediction for the halo mass function is numerically identical to the Press–Schechter prediction in the same cosmology. There is no first-order discriminator from the linear regime alone.

The chapter therefore performs a consistency check, not a novel prediction. The interesting work is in the bookkeeping — which of the four Ch 8 components actually clusters, and why — and in the honest statement of what the framework cannot say in the nonlinear regime. The chapter is not a survey of structure formation, and it is not a triumph: it is a careful accounting of where the framework's predictive grasp ends.

A note for the Skeptic reviewer. The hardest question for this chapter is: isn't this just LambdaCDM with relabeled parameters? The honest answer has two parts. First, in the linear regime, yes: the framework's growth factor, power spectrum, and halo mass function coincide numerically with the LambdaCDM predictions in the strict w sub A = -1 canonical case. The Skeptic is correct that the chapter, taken in isolation, cannot distinguish the framework from LambdaCDM. Second, however, the parameters are not relabeled — they are derived in chapter 8 from non-cosmological inputs. The framework predicts the same observables as LambdaCDM but from a different chain. Whether one finds this interesting or uninteresting depends on whether one cares about the shape of the chain or only about the leaf at the end of it. The Skeptic should be interested in the next chapter (Ch 11), which catalogs the discriminators that the framework's identification Omega sub B = Waters Below makes available beyond what LambdaCDM offers, and in Vol 6, which compiles the predictions catalog. §10.13 itemizes the chapter's load-bearing claims.

A note for the Physicist reviewer. The hardest question for the Physicist is: is the linear growth equation genuinely derived from the framework, or are you smuggling in LambdaCDM by writing it in standard form? The chain is: the linearized fluid equations of Vol 3 Ch 5 (continuity + Euler + Poisson, valid for any non-relativistic self-gravitating fluid on a curved background) are applied to the brane matter density rho sub m = rho sub b + rho sub B — where rho sub b are the baryons (5 percent of rho  crit) and rho sub B is the brane-projected Waters Below field (27 percent of rho  crit, derived in Vol 1 §6.7 and inherited through Ch 8). The background H(a) is the chapter-8 E(z). The Vol 1 Ch 5 brane is taken as the spatial slice on which the perturbation lives, but at the scales of interest (k much greater than H, well within the Hubble horizon, well above the brane thickness) all of the brane geometry projects out and one is left with the Newtonian limit on a comoving FLRW background. The Vol 1 Ch 6 equation of state of the Waters Below — w sub B approximately equal to 0, sound speed c sub s,B much less than c — is what makes Waters Below cluster identically to cold dark matter. None of this is reinvented for this chapter. What is particular to the framework is the content of rho sub m: it is baryons plus Waters Below, not baryons plus a hypothetical CDM particle. The growth equation does not care what the matter is made of; it cares only about rho sub m and the equation of state. So the equation is the same; the matter is different.

Radiation does not cluster on subhorizon scales.

The Jeans length of a fluid is lambda sub J = c sub s square root of pi /G rho, with c sub s the sound speed. For the radiation component, w sub r = 1/3 and c sub s = c/ square root of 3, giving a Jeans length of order the Hubble radius. Perturbations in the radiation fluid on scales lambda < lambda sub J propagate as acoustic waves rather than collapsing — they oscillate. At any subhorizon wavenumber k > k sub J approximately H/c, the radiation density contrast delta sub r oscillates around zero with a slowly decaying amplitude (the prerecombination acoustic oscillations of chapter 9, in fact). Radiation does not contribute to the source term in (5.10.7) on the scales where matter clusters.

The chapter has nothing more to say about radiation. After recombination, Omega sub r is so small (approximately 10 to the -4) that it would not matter even if it did cluster.

Waters Above does not cluster on subhorizon scales.

This is the framework-specific argument and deserves more care. The strict canonical result w sub A = -1 is derived in Vol 1 Ch 6 (specifically §6.5) from the brane-projected action of the Waters Above field and is not assumed here. By that derivation, the Waters Above field Psi sub A has equation of state w sub A approximately equal to -1 on the brane projection and a relaxation time of order the Hubble time. A subhorizon spatial perturbation delta Psi sub A( x, t) obeys an effective wave equation with wave speed c and damping rate H; for k much greater than H/c the perturbation decays on a sound-crossing time approximately 1/(kc), which is much shorter than a Hubble time. To leading order in the cosmological perturbation expansion, delta Psi sub A = 0 on subhorizon scales.

Equivalently, in the language of effective fluid dynamics, the Waters Above sound speed equals c (a consequence of w = -1 in the strict canonical case), so the Jeans length is the Hubble radius and there are no clustering modes inside it.

What the Waters Above does instead is shape the background expansion: it enters H(a) through the constant Omega sub A in (5.10.1). This affects the growth equation (5.10.8) through the E'(a)/E(a) Hubble drag term and the Omega sub m(a) = Omega sub m a to the -3/E to the 2(a) source term. The framework's claim is that this is not a coincidence: the Waters Above is what cosmologists call the cosmological constant, and its dynamics are by construction the dynamics that produce the freezing.

Bullet Cluster: A Qualitative Discriminator.

The Bullet Cluster (1E 0657-56) is the canonical observation that something gravitating in clusters is collisionless on a cluster crossing time. In the merger of two galaxy clusters, the gas (collisional, electromagnetic) is shocked to a stop near the impact center, while the gravitating mass — measured by weak gravitational lensing — passes through and lies displaced from the gas peaks. The displacement is roughly a cluster radius and is now measured in several merging-cluster systems beyond the original Bullet (MACS J0025.4-1222, Abell 520, the "Train Wreck" of Abell 520, and others).

The Bullet Cluster is the observation that, more than any other single piece of evidence, ruled out the proposal that what cosmologists call "dark matter" might be a modification of gravity (MOND and its descendants). It demonstrated, almost in cartoon form, that there is something there that has mass and does not have a strong electromagnetic cross section.

In the framework, this is consistent with the Vol 1 Ch 6 derivation of the Waters Below self-coupling.
