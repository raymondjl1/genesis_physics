## Mission

*This chapter compiles every known gap in the Genesis Physics framework — the quantum-sector blockers, the precision shortfalls, the speculation-heavy technology validations, the consciousness unknowns, and the cross-cutting program-level issues — into a single honest catalogue, and frames each as a research invitation specific enough that a competent graduate student could choose one and start a dissertation on the following day. It is also the chapter in which the framework formally responds to the outstanding critic and skeptic reports on record.*

## "Why" Chain

1. **Why a dedicated open-problems chapter if Vols 1–5 and Ch 1–13 of Vol 6 have already acknowledged specific gaps in situ?** — Because in-situ acknowledgement is helpful for the reader of that chapter but useless for a graduate student looking for a thesis. The open problems are scattered across six volumes, with the heaviest concentration in Vol 4 (quantum sector); a prospective contributor has no way to see the landscape. Ch 14 is that landscape — one map, all the craters, each scaled to how much work it would take to fill.

2. **Why use "BLOCKER / HIGH / MEDIUM / LOW" severity labels if the framework's strongest position is that these are invitations?** — Because a reader — especially a student choosing between problems — needs to know which problems, if solved, would most strengthen the framework. The severity label is not a shame tag. It is a leverage tag. A BLOCKER-class problem like the spin-1/2 fermion derivation is not more shameful than a MEDIUM-class N-body precision problem; it is more *valuable* to solve. The severity classification tells a student whose attack on which problem would most reward the framework — and, equally important, whose null result would most constrain it.

3. **Why include formal responses to the critic report and skeptic analysis in this chapter rather than as stand-alone peer-review appendices?** — Because the critic and skeptic reports *are* the external view of the open-problems landscape. The critic identified a 76-order-of-magnitude error in the Firmament tension calculation (since provisionally resolved, in a way this chapter documents); a perpetual-motion risk on the MRG thermodynamics (still partly open); a cosmic-capacitor factor-of-20 discrepancy (since corrected). The skeptic identified a piezo-at-GHz impossibility (resolved by rectenna substitution), a Casimir pressure overstatement (corrected), and a load-bearing replenishment efficiency η that remains the single most consequential open parameter in the entire technology program. Responding in the open-problems chapter makes the peer-review feedback load-bearing for the research program; responding in an appendix buries it.

4. **Why write the open problems in the five-field anatomy (known / missing / path / meaning / difficulty) rather than as prose?** — Because a graduate student reading this chapter is performing a search: "which of these problems matches the instruments available in my lab, my theoretical strengths, and the time I can afford?" The five-field anatomy supports search. Prose does not. A reader skimming the difficulty field alone should be able to cull the list by an order of magnitude before reading the detail.

5. **Why is "what a resolution would mean" a required field?** — Because it is the difference between a problem and a chore. Chores are laboriously solved; problems are ambitiously attacked. A problem whose resolution would generate a novel prediction, close a BLOCKER, retire a speculative technology path, or force a program-level commitment is worth more attention than a problem whose resolution would merely tidy a derivation. The "what it would mean" field forces the framework to state what it would pay for the solution. If it pays nothing, the problem is decoration. No decorations are listed here.

6. **Why keep predictions (P-XXX) out of this chapter?** — Because predictions are what the framework *claims* to know; open problems are what the framework *acknowledges it does not*. Mixing them corrupts both catalogues. When a resolution to an open problem would generate a new prediction, we say so, and we flag the prediction as contingent. We do not enter contingent predictions into the P-numbered registry. That registry belongs to Vol 6 Ch 1–13 and the master index in Appendix A.

7. **Why does the chapter hand off to Ch 15 rather than simply closing?** — Because many of the open problems are shared. String theory does not solve the hierarchy problem either; loop quantum gravity has not derived the fine structure constant; causal set theory has not produced a unique vacuum selection. Where the Genesis Physics open problem overlaps with an open problem in a competing program, the cross-program leverage is available and should be used — a student might attack the same problem using our language and theirs in parallel, learn from both, and contribute to both. Ch 15 picks up where Ch 14 ends, specifically by naming which open problems are shared and which are not.

## Section Outline

### Section 14.1: Introduction — An Honest Catalogue
- Topic: Why the framework keeps a master open-problems list; the discipline it enforces.
- Why: Scattered acknowledgements help individual chapters; a landscape helps prospective contributors.
- Content: Philosophical framing (invitations vs. embarrassments); the five-field anatomy; what the chapter is and is not; the severity scale; the structure of the chapter.

### Section 14.2: Methodology — How Problems Were Chosen, Scored, and Organized
- Topic: Selection, scoring, and organization criteria.
- Why: Transparency about what made the list and what didn't.
- Content: Selection criteria; severity definitions (BLOCKER / HIGH / MEDIUM / LOW); difficulty definitions (Master's / PhD / Multi-generational); five-field anatomy specification; the landscape figure (Fig 6.14.1); explicit exclusions (a note on what did not make the list and why).

### Section 14.3: BLOCKER — OP-1 Spin-½ Fermions from Bosonic Membrane
- Topic: The single BLOCKER-class problem in the framework.
- Why: If fermionic statistics cannot be derived from a bosonic membrane, the entire matter sector is on borrowed structure.
- Content: Full five-field anatomy; three candidate resolution paths (supersymmetric extension; topological Z₂ charge from membrane ribbon structure; imposed Grassmann sector with derivation-from-geometry); what a resolution would mean; the relation to Vol 4 Ch 6; the relation to string-theory's NSR formalism.

### Section 14.4: HIGH — Quantum Sector Precision
- Topic: The five HIGH-severity quantum problems.
- Why: These are the calibration problems — not whether the framework has the right structure, but whether it has the right numbers.
- Content: OP-2 mass spectrum 1000×; OP-3 weak interaction / CP violation completeness; OP-4 Higgs mechanism completeness; OP-5 Yukawa coupling derivation; OP-6 fine structure constant final precision. Each with full anatomy.

### Section 14.5: MEDIUM — Classical, Gravitational, and Technology Problems
- Topic: Seven MEDIUM-severity problems spanning classical physics, gravity, and the speculative-technology chapters of Vol 6.
- Why: These are the mature-framework problems — where the physics is right enough that only precision and validation remain.
- Content: OP-7 running couplings; OP-8 N-body completeness; OP-9 GR observables precision; OP-10 FTL causality containment; OP-11 MRG experimental validation pathway; OP-12 life detection sensitivity; OP-13 QED loops from membrane vacuum.

### Section 14.6: LOW — Parameter-Level Open Problems
- Topic: Six LOW-severity problems mostly about fitting remaining free parameters.
- Why: These are the bookkeeping problems — solvable with existing tools if existing people are available to do the work.
- Content: OP-14 PMNS; OP-15 CKM; OP-16 ν mass hierarchy; OP-17 η (replenishment efficiency); OP-18 BCS from membrane condensed matter; OP-19 absolute scales ℏ, G, k_B (which would graduate to HIGH if the 1000× problem weren't already subsuming them).

### Section 14.7: INHERITED — Consciousness Open Problems (OP-13.1 through OP-13.8)
- Topic: The eight consciousness open problems inherited from Ch 13 §13.9.
- Why: These problems are load-bearing for Ch 9, Ch 11, Ch 12, and Ch 13; the master catalogue owes them space.
- Content: Brief five-field anatomies for OP-20 through OP-27 (controllability; Firmament-side neural correlate; decoherence time scaling; Ψ_spirit ontology; relation to other consciousness theories; phenomenology bridge; artificial systems; death/sleep/discontinuities). Each with one paragraph pointing back to Ch 13 §13.9 for the full treatment.

### Section 14.8: Cross-Cutting — Program-Level Open Problems
- Topic: Three issues that live above individual OPs and shape the whole program.
- Why: Some open problems are structural; ignoring them risks misallocating effort on the specific OPs.
- Content: (a) Absolute scale determination across the framework (mass, length, energy): how many of OP-2, OP-19 are facets of one meta-problem? (b) The "imported mathematics vs. imported physics" distinction: where the framework tolerates borrowed mathematics (Green's functions, PDE theory) vs. where it must derive (Schwinger formula, BCS): clarifying the line. (c) The observational-constraint program: which OPs, if solved, would generate new predictions outside the existing 153-entry catalogue, and which would merely tighten existing predictions.

### Section 14.9: Formal Response to the Critic Report
- Topic: Complete, point-by-point response to `critic_report.md`.
- Why: Peer review is load-bearing for the open-problems landscape; burying it in an appendix misallocates its weight.
- Content: Executive summary of the original critic report; each of the critic's eight claims restated; the framework's current status on each (RESOLVED / PARTIAL / OPEN / DISPUTED), with evidence; the critic's strongest finding (fine structure constant SOLID — α⁻¹ = 137.15 within 0.12%) affirmed; the critic's FATAL finding (Firmament tension σ 76 orders of magnitude off) reported as RESOLVED with source (the FINE_STRUCTURE_DERIVATION.md treats σ correctly via the 6D Green's function asymptotic; the critic's attempted independent derivation used an inappropriate formula); the critic's CRITICAL findings (thermodynamic replenishment model; orifice flow; cosmic capacitor factor-of-20) reported as PARTIAL with OP numbers; the critic's PROBLEMATIC findings mapped to OPs where appropriate; explicit acknowledgement that the critic's recommendations in Section 10 (IMMEDIATE / MEDIUM-TERM / COMMUNICATION STRATEGY) are substantially adopted in the Vol 6 writing plan. Fig 6.14.3 summary.

### Section 14.10: Formal Response to the Skeptic Analysis
- Topic: Complete, point-by-point response to `skeptic_analysis.md` (the iterated version).
- Why: The skeptic analysis identified engineering-level impossibilities (piezo at GHz; 154 kW from a soda can); confirming their resolution publicly is both honest and instructive.
- Content: Iteration history acknowledged; each fixed item (Casimir pressure 13 Pa at 100 nm not 10⁵ Pa; rectenna substitution for piezo; power estimate corrected from 154 kW to ~36 W net; thermal ΔT 53°C acceptable) confirmed; each WHAT'S UNCERTAIN item indexed against OPs (replenishment efficiency η → OP-17; magnetic bias effectiveness → OP-11 sub-item; orientation dependence → OP-11 sub-item; 3D NAND geometry mapping → OP-11 sub-item; peak-vs-RMS power → OP-11 sub-item); the skeptic's five WHAT'S SOLID items (Casimir formula; dynamic Casimir; BaTiO₃ properties; rectenna GHz; fine-structure) affirmed as framework-consistent; the skeptic's closing verdict ("build it and test it. Phase 1 costs $150 and answers the question definitively") adopted as the framework's own position on OP-11. Fig 6.14.4 summary. A note on the theological-motivation vs. physics-argument discipline: the skeptic's closing paragraph included a theological argument for η > 0; the framework records the argument as motivation, not as derivation, and says so.

### Section 14.11: Prioritization Matrix — Which Problems First, Who Attacks Them, What a Program Would Cost
- Topic: Concrete guidance for allocating effort across the 27 OPs.
- Why: A landscape without a direction is a catalogue; a catalogue with directions is a research program.
- Content: Two-axis chart (Fig 6.14.5): effort in person-years (x) × impact on framework (y), with each OP plotted. Four quadrants: Quick Wins (low effort, high impact — OP-17 replenishment efficiency; OP-11 MRG Phase 1); Strategic Investments (high effort, high impact — OP-1 spin-½; OP-6 fine-structure coefficient; OP-2 mass spectrum); Thesis Topics (medium effort, medium impact — OP-14 PMNS; OP-15 CKM; OP-8 N-body completeness); Decorative Problems (low impact — explicitly, there are none listed; the matrix shows the absence). Estimated person-year budgets for each severity tier; institutional capability mapping (which labs, which theory groups); explicit statement: the framework is willing to re-sever a problem downward (HIGH → MEDIUM) if new evidence warrants, and upward (MEDIUM → HIGH) if gaps deepen; a reminder that severity is a tag on the current landscape, not a permanent judgment.

### Section 14.12: Chapter Summary and Handoff to Ch 15
- Topic: Synthesis, chapter-end problem set, handoff.
- Content: One-paragraph synthesis (the framework has 27 open problems, one BLOCKER, five HIGH, seven MEDIUM, six LOW, eight INHERITED from Ch 13; the critic's and skeptic's reports have been addressed; the prioritization matrix is available; the open-problems landscape is published); chapter-end problem set (10 problems — 3 computational, 4 conceptual, 3 challenge); handoff to Ch 15 (Connections to Other Programs) explicitly flagging which OPs are shared with string theory (OP-1 spin-½, OP-2 mass spectrum, OP-15 CKM), loop quantum gravity (OP-9 GR precision), causal set theory (OP-6 coefficient 1.44), and constructor theory (OP-1 spin-½); closing remark: these problems are the program's best argument that it is alive and inviting work, not a finished system defending itself.
