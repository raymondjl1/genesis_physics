## Pedagogical Design

### Problem Design Philosophy

Following Feynman's principle: "If you cannot explain something clearly, you do not understand it," each problem is designed to teach, not merely test.

**[C] Computational Problems** develop calculation skills and mathematical fluency:
- Direct application of formulas from the chapter
- Dimensional analysis and unit conversions
- Numerical evaluation with realistic parameters
- Building intuition for scale and magnitude
- Strengthening command of mathematical tools

**[W] "Why" Conceptual Problems** deepen understanding of principles:
- Qualitative reasoning and physical intuition
- Relationships between concepts
- Why certain choices (6D, not 4D; $w = -1$, not $w = 0$)
- Implications and consequences of axioms
- Connections to broader contexts (theology, philosophy, observational astronomy)

**[X] Challenge Problems** promote synthesis and creativity:
- Open-ended design tasks ("propose a mechanism")
- Thought experiments and scenarios
- Integration across multiple chapters
- Speculation within physical constraints
- Connection between the mathematical framework and theological grounding

### Cross-Chapter Threading

Problems systematically build on earlier chapters:
- **Chapter 1** establishes axioms and nomenclature (foundation)
- **Chapter 2** provides mathematical machinery (tools)
- **Chapter 3** applies mathematics to zone manifold (structure)
- **Chapter 4** studies extra dimensions (geometry)
- **Chapter 5** analyzes the Firmament (boundary)
- **Chapter 6** solves field equations (dynamics)

Forward references are **avoided strictly**: each problem uses only material from the current chapter and prior chapters.

### Solution Coverage

Each chapter includes selected solutions for ~20% of problems:
- **Computational**: Simple calculations shown in full, building confidence
- **Conceptual**: Reasoning explained, emphasizing the "why"
- **Challenge**: Outlines and key insights, leaving room for individual development

Solutions model rigorous communication and mathematical exposition.

## Usage Recommendations

### For Students

1. **First pass**: Work [C] problems to build computational competence
2. **Second pass**: Tackle [W] problems to consolidate understanding
3. **Third pass**: Engage [X] problems to develop mastery and insight
4. **Review solutions** after attempting problems (not before)
5. **Discuss challenge problems** with peers and instructors for multiple perspectives

### For Instructors

- **Week 1–2**: Assign [C] problems (1–3 per class) for fluency
- **Week 2–3**: Mix [C] and [W] for homework; discuss [W] in class
- **Week 3–4**: Introduce [X] problems as discussion topics and deeper dives
- **Midterm prep**: Review all solutions together, highlighting connections
- **Final prep**: Have students propose extensions or alternative scenarios inspired by [X] problems

### For Self-Study

- Work through all problems sequentially within each chapter
- Consult solutions **after** genuine effort
- Keep a problem log noting key insights and remaining questions
- Use challenge problems to guide further reading or exploration
