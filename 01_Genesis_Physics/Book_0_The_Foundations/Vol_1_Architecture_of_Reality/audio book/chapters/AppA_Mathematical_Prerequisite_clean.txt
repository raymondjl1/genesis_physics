Introduction.

You've seen this mathematics before. You may not remember it all. This appendix is not a tutorial—it's a cheat sheet for a graduate physicist who needs to unstick themselves without leaving the page.

Each topic gives you:
- The idea — what this thing does and why we care
- The notation — the symbols, what they mean
- The key formulas — the tools you'll actually use
- Where it shows up — which chapter uses this, so you know why you're remembering it

Read straight through before you start, or jump to what you need when you need it. If you want proofs and derivations, go read a proper textbook. Here, we move fast.

The idea: A manifold is a space that looks locally like Euclidean space, but globally might be bent or twisted. You can't use a single (x, y, z) everywhere; you need charts.

Definition:

- Every point has a neighborhood homeomorphic to R to the n

The fundamental group pi sub 1(M, p sub 0) is the set of homotopy equivalence classes of loops, with the group operation being concatenation: the expression (go around gamma sub 1, then gamma sub 2).

Examples:
- pi sub 1( R to the n) = e (trivial; all loops shrink)
- pi sub 1(S to the 1) = Z (integers; loops wind around the circle)
- pi sub 1(S to the 2) = e (trivial; sphere is simply connected)
- pi sub 1(S to the 1 times S to the 1) = Z times Z (torus has two independent winding directions)

Used in: Chapter 2 (mathematical structure), Chapter 3 (zone manifold topology).

Homotopy Groups.

The idea: Generalize pi sub 1. Instead of loops, use maps from n-dimensional spheres. These groups capture higher-dimensional "holes" in a space.

Definition:

The n-th homotopy group pi sub n(M, p sub 0) is the set of homotopy classes of continuous maps f: S to the n to M with f( basepoint) = p sub 0.

Examples:
- pi sub 0(M) = connected components of M (not quite a group, but a set)
- pi sub 1(M) = fundamental group (loops)
- pi sub 2(M) = maps of 2-spheres
- pi sub 3(S to the 2) = Z (Hopf fibration; famous example)

Intuition: pi sub n detects n-dimensional "holes."

Used in: Chapter 3 (zone manifold structure—checking connectivity and topological constraints).
