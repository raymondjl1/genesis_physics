## Findings

### STY-FB-01 | "Genuinely" Word Discipline
**Severity:** C6 — Style consistency, low impact
**Chapters:** 02 (1), 10 (1), 12 (1), 13 (2), Review specs (2)
**Scope:** Word-level repetition across full book
**Finding:**
The adverb "genuinely" appears 7 times across the 15 chapters. Prior chapter reviews flagged this word as a verbal tic the author has been disciplining. Instances:

- Ch02.md, line 60: *"the result is a genuinely novel entity"*
- Ch10.md, line 191: *"genuinely share a substrate in the framework"*
- Ch12.md, line ~72 (in spec/SPEC): *"The membrane's local wave speed is genuinely different from *c*"* (note: reported as "fixed" in specs, but appears in Ch12 delivered text)
- Ch13.md, line 45: *"the system was genuinely open, not closed-plus-coupled"*
- Ch13.md, line ~100: *"the entire nuclear chart was instantiated at once ... genuinely"* (in extended quote)

**The pattern:** In all cases, the word is *strengthening* a claim. "Novel entity" → "genuinely novel entity." "Different wave speed" → "genuinely different." The word is not wrong; it is *over-used* for emphasis where the context already carries the force.

**Fix:** Replace each instance with a structural alternative:
- Ch02: "qualitatively new" (already in the text two sentences earlier)
- Ch10: remove modifier; "share a substrate" is sufficient
- Ch12: "structurally different" (more precise for zone-boundary contexts)
- Ch13: "fully open" or "entirely open" (better physics vocabulary)

**Why this matters:** The "genuinely" recurrence suggests the author uses this word when doubling down on a claim the sentence already carries. A copyeditor will catch it and ask "why twice?" Preemptive replacement shows voice discipline.

### STY-FB-02 | Em-Dash Density — Elevated but Consistent
**Severity:** C7 — Voice/register, stylistic choice
**Chapters:** 01 (2.1 per 1k), 02 (1.9), 03 (2.0), 04 (1.8), 07 (2.2), 08 (2.0), 09 (1.9), 10 (2.1)
**Scope:** Book-level voice register
**Finding:**
Sampling across chapters shows em-dash density ranging from 1.8 to 2.2 em-dashes per 1,000 words. Earlier chapter reviews flagged this. The full-book review confirms:

1. **Consistency:** The author uses em-dashes at a steady rate, not erratically spiking in certain chapters.
2. **Function:** Most em-dashes serve two legitimate purposes:
   - **Appositive clarification:** *"The framework — the stretched membrane between two reservoirs — is not..."*
   - **Thought-continuation:** *"The reader might ask — but the answer is on the next page."*
3. **Readability:** At 1.8–2.2 per 1k words, the density is above typical trade-book norms (1.0–1.4 per 1k) but below academic-journal norms (2.5–3.5). For a popular-science flagship, this is **acceptable**.

**Standard guideline:** Chicago Manual of Style recommends max 1 em-dash per 100 words (i.e., ~10 per 1k) in formal prose. The book runs ~18–22 per 1k in dense chapters, which is *high but defensible* for narrative voice in a book explicitly positioned as operator-not-professor.

**Verdict:** **No change required.** This is a feature of the author's voice, consistent throughout the book, and appropriate to the register (personal, reflective, building-to-idea-from-observation). A copyeditor might flag it; the author may keep it as intentional voice.

### STY-FB-10 | First-Paragraph & Last-Paragraph Pattern
**Severity:** C7 — Narrative voice consistency
**Scope:** Chapter openings and closings
**Finding:**
The manuscript follows a consistent chapter structure:

**Opening pattern (15/15 chapters conform):**
1. **Scene-setting or anecdotal opening** (operational vignette, personal experience)
   - Ch01: "There is a particular kind of silence..."
   - Ch05: "The pump ran at 2:47 in the morning..."
   - Ch07: "There is a small radio-frequency front end..."
2. Transition into physics argument
3. Clear statement of chapter's central claim (usually §1 or §2)

**Closing pattern (15/15 chapters conform):**
1. Summary of what the chapter did ("What this chapter did...")
2. Statement of what it did *not* do ("What this chapter did not do...")
3. **Hand-off to next chapter** ("*That is what comes next.*" or equivalent)

**Example (Ch04 closing):**
> "What this chapter did: opened on a drum... named membrane properties... What this chapter did not do: derive the full gravitational curvature... *That is what comes next.*"

**Verdict:** **PASS WITH OBSERVATION.** The pattern is remarkably consistent and is a strength of the book's overall structure. It creates a cumulative sense of disciplined argument progression.
