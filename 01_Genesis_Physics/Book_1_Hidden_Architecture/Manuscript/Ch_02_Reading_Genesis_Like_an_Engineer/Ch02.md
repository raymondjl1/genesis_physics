# Chapter 2
## Reading Genesis Like an Engineer

---

There is a particular smell to a thick stack of engineering documents, and anyone who has spent time in aerospace knows it. A mix of toner, binder glue, and the faint metallic tang of a room whose HVAC has been running for too long on recycled air. I have read more pages in rooms like that than I am willing to count. Interface control documents. System specifications. Statements of work. Certification packages. Hazard analyses. Requirements traces long enough that you could roll them out across the floor of a hangar and still not see the end.

People outside the industry sometimes assume the reading is a formality. It isn't. The reading is the job. The airframe does what the document says the airframe does, or the airframe doesn't fly. A sloppy reader of an interface control document is how a multi-million-dollar box ends up bolted to a structure it cannot actually talk to, and the program finds out about it on the test stand at two o'clock in the morning. I have seen that. I have been, once or twice, the reason it didn't happen, and I have been, once or twice, the reason I had to call someone at two in the morning to explain what was about to go wrong. The reading is the job.

The specific discipline I was trained into, and later spent nine years at Boeing Insitu teaching to younger engineers, is called Model-Based Systems Engineering. The name is heavier than it needs to be. The practice is straightforward. When you open a specification document, you read it three ways at once. You read the *nouns* — the components, the parts, the subsystems. You read the *verbs* — the operations the document prescribes on those components. And you read the *prepositions and boundary words* — *between*, *across*, *above*, *below*, *into*, *from* — which tell you how the components relate to each other and where the interfaces live. You circle nouns. You underline verbs. You draw arrows from boundary words to architectural diagrams in the margin. And after you have done that on a few dozen pages, you have a picture in your head of the system the document is describing, whether the document ever gave you a picture or not.

It is an old discipline. It predates MBSE by a long way. Engineers have been reading drawings and specifications like this since there were drawings and specifications. What MBSE added, in the last couple of decades, was the insistence that every architectural claim in the document trace somewhere — to a requirement, to an axiom, to a test case — and that no claim be allowed to hang in the air unsupported. In the program office, *always answer why* is not a bumper sticker. It is how I have kept programs alive.

I bring all of that up because in Chapter 1 I left us at a kitchen table with a document in front of me, and I told you I had started to read that document in a way I had never quite read it before. I did not tell you, then, what it was. This chapter is where I tell you.

The document was Genesis 1.

I need you to read the next few pages carefully, because a lot depends on what I mean by *this chapter is where I tell you,* and a lot more depends on what I do *not* mean. I am not about to do theology. I am not about to do apologetics. I am not about to do a literary close reading, and I am certainly not about to do the kind of thing where somebody claims the ancient Hebrews knew about quantum mechanics. None of that is what this chapter is. What this chapter is, instead, is a demonstration of what happens when you take the discipline I just described — the MBSE, the read-it-like-a-spec discipline I do for a living — and you apply it to the first chapter of the book most people in the Western world have heard of and almost nobody I know has ever read this particular way.

What happens, it turns out, is that the document reads like a specification.

Notice what I am and am not saying there. I am not saying Genesis 1 *is* an engineering specification in the sense that some ancient Hebrew systems engineer sat down at a drafting table and wrote one. I am saying that when you apply the reading discipline to it, the document behaves the way specifications behave. It has a top-level system decomposition. It has named components. It uses two different verbs for two different kinds of creative operation, and it uses them consistently. It describes a load-bearing structural member. It names two distinct fields on either side of that structural member. And it lays out a seven-stage sequence of operations that turn a featureless initial state into a completed system.

That is not a theological claim on my part. That is what the document *says,* read the way I read every other document that crosses my desk. You may, at the end of this chapter, decide that the reading is a coincidence — that the ancient writers happened to use vocabulary that sounds like an engineer's when you squint. That is a fair response. I am not, in this chapter, going to try to convince you otherwise. What I am going to do is walk you slowly through the reading itself, and let you decide.

I want to walk you through four Hebrew words. I am not going to try to make you a Hebrew scholar. I am an aerospace engineer who had to teach himself enough of the language to read the original for himself, and I want to be clear up front that I am not a theologian and I am not a professor of Semitic languages. What I have done is look at the words carefully, in the lexicons used by actual Hebrew scholars — Brown-Driver-Briggs, HALOT, Gesenius — and I will give you the four that do the architectural work. Four. Not forty. And I will give each one to you in Hebrew script the first time it appears, so you can see it with your own eyes, and in transliteration thereafter so you can say it without getting your tongue in a knot. The four words are *bara*, *asah*, *raqia*, and *mayim*. By the end of this chapter you will be on speaking terms with all four, and that is all the Hebrew you will need to follow the rest of the book.

---

Let's start where the document starts.

The first sentence of Genesis, in a clean English rendering, is: *In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth.* Twelve English words. Every reader of this book has heard them. Very few of them have been asked to read them as if they were the opening line of a technical document.

Do me a favor. Pretend, for the length of the next three paragraphs, that someone has just handed you the opening line of an aircraft-system specification and you are the engineer tasked with building a mental model of the system the document is describing. You have not been told anything else. You are reading cold. What do you see?

You see three entities named. *The beginning.* *The heavens.* *The earth.* And you see one verb. *Created.* Four words doing the work.

Now, in the Hebrew, two of those three entities are technically more interesting than they look in English. *The beginning* is the Hebrew word *bereshit* (בְּרֵאשִׁית). An English reader looks at *in the beginning* and hears "at some point on a timeline that already existed." The Hebrew does not say that. The Hebrew is an absolute construct — *in beginning,* not *in the beginning* — with no definite article. Grammatically, the sentence is not placing a creative act somewhere on a pre-existing clock. It is saying that the clock itself, the beginning itself, is what the document is about to describe coming into existence. Time itself starts here. An engineer reading a specification that does not assume a pre-existing clock would stop and circle that. The document is telling you that one of the things about to be created is the timeline on which everything else will happen.

*The heavens* is the Hebrew word *shamayim*. In Hebrew, *shamayim* is always plural. There is no singular. Whatever the document is describing, it is not one thing called *the sky* — it is a layered concept, multiple regions stacked, all bundled under one noun. An engineer reading *the heavens* in this sentence would not yet know what those layers are. An engineer *would* know, however, that the document had just committed to describing a system with stacked regions. Circle that.

*The earth* is the Hebrew word *eretz*. *Eretz* is context-dependent in a way most English nouns are not. It can mean *planet earth*, *dry land as opposed to sea*, *territory*, or *ground*. In this particular verse — the opening sentence, before anything else exists — *eretz* cannot mean "the planet" in the astronomical sense, because the astronomical apparatus is not set up yet. The word is functioning here as the name of a whole domain, a whole region of whatever has just been created, paired with *the heavens* as the other region. You see this construction — *the heavens and the earth* — in a few other places in the Hebrew Bible, and it is a standard merism: two terms together that name *the totality,* the way "young and old" can name everyone or "heaven and hell" can name everywhere a Christian imagination pins a destination.

So the first sentence, read as a spec, commits the document to describing a totality — divided into two named regions, one of them inherently layered — brought into existence along with the timeline on which the rest of the document will operate.

Circle four nouns. One verb. Three architectural facts.

And the verb, as it happens, is the first word that carries real architectural weight.

---

The verb in that first sentence is *bara* (בָּרָא). It is the word English Bibles translate as *created,* and it is the word around which I want to spend this section.

*Bara* is not the only word for *make* or *create* in biblical Hebrew. There are several. The two that matter for reading Genesis 1 are *bara* and *asah* (עָשָׂה). In English they both come out as *made* or *created* without much distinction. In Hebrew, they are not the same verb. They are not interchangeable. And the document of Genesis 1 uses them consistently, for two different kinds of creative act, in ways that I will now argue are not accidental.

*Bara* is a rare verb, grammatically speaking. In the entire Hebrew Bible, it takes God as its subject and God alone. Humans never *bara*. Kings never *bara*. Craftsmen never *bara*. The verb is reserved. Its usage range is narrow, and what it names — according to the standard lexicons I named earlier, Brown-Driver-Briggs and HALOT, and confirmed in Wenham's *Genesis 1–15* commentary — is the creation of something qualitatively new, a new category, a thing that was not before. It may or may not use an existing substrate; the point of the verb is not where the material came from, but that the result is a genuinely novel entity.

*Asah* is a different animal. *Asah* is one of the most common verbs in Biblical Hebrew. It means to fashion, to make, to form, to construct — operations performed on existing materials. Humans *asah* houses. Cooks *asah* meals. Craftsmen *asah* tools. It is the verb of manufacturing. If I am in a shop and I take a piece of stock and I machine it into a finished part, that, in biblical Hebrew, is *asah*. If I take a blank canvas and I paint a landscape on it, that is *asah*. *Asah* operates on what is already there.

Now watch what the document of Genesis 1 does with these two verbs. It uses *bara* in the opening sentence — the heavens and the earth *bara* into existence. It uses *bara* again on day five, when the first animate-life creatures — the Hebrew phrase is *nephesh chayah,* often glossed as *living soul* or *living creature* — come into being. And it uses *bara* one more time, three times in a single verse, on day six, when humans are described as being created in the image of God. That is the full distribution of *bara* in Genesis 1. Three moments. Everywhere else in the seven-stage sequence, where the document narrates a making operation, the verb is *asah.*

An engineer reading this pattern does not blur it. In a specification document, when the author consistently uses two different verbs for two different acts, you take the author at their word. The document is telling you that three moments in the sequence are categorically different from the others — three moments when a qualitatively new thing is brought into being, as opposed to fashioned from material already present. The document names the three. Opening: the heavens and the earth themselves. Day five: sentient life. Day six: humans, emphasized by triple repetition of the verb.

I am not going to tell you what those three moments *mean* in this chapter. I am not going to argue for a mass-creation mechanism or a consciousness mechanism or an imago-dei mechanism. That is the work of later chapters — Chapter 9 for where matter comes from, later chapters for the emergence of consciousness. My job in this chapter is to let you notice the pattern the document is making and to refuse to blur it on your behalf.

One honest concession, because the voice of this book requires it. There are serious Hebrew scholars who read *bara* and *asah* as near-synonyms, and who read the pattern I just described as ordinary literary variation rather than load-bearing vocabulary. That is a defensible reading. I do not share it. The case for treating the distinction as real — which the full framework needs — gets made rigorously in Chapter 8 and, for the math-hungry reader, in Foundations Volume 1, Chapter 1, where the Hebrew vocabulary is mapped to the formal axioms of the framework. In this chapter I am only asking you to notice that the pattern exists. The argument for what it implies comes later.

Two Hebrew verbs. Two kinds of making. Three moments where the rarer verb shows up. Circle all of them.

---

The third word I want to walk you to is, for my money, the single most under-translated word in the Hebrew Bible. It is the word behind what English translations give you as *firmament,* or *expanse,* or *vault,* or *dome,* or occasionally *sky,* depending on which committee did the translation and what century they were working in. None of those English words is a good rendering. Every one of them picks up some fraction of what the Hebrew word actually says. None of them picks up the whole of it.

The word is *raqia* (רָקִיעַ). It appears for the first time on day two of the sequence, in verses six through eight, where the document says *let there be a raqia in the midst of the waters, and let it divide the waters from the waters.*

Here is the thing about *raqia* that almost nobody is told in Sunday school. The noun comes from a verb. The verb is *raqa* (רָקַע). And *raqa,* in biblical Hebrew, means *to hammer out, to beat flat, to stretch, to extend something under tension the way a metalsmith flattens gold leaf.* It is a workshop verb. When the Hebrew Bible describes a craftsman beating gold into a thin sheet for the tabernacle furniture, the verb it uses is *raqa.* When the Psalms describe the spreading of the earth over the waters, the verb it uses is *raqa.* It is the verb of stretching something flat and taut. It is not the verb of building a dome. It is not the verb of conjuring a sky. It is the verb of hammering.

So when the document gets to day two (Genesis 1:6-8) and says *let there be a raqia,* the first thing the etymology tells an engineer is that the object being named is derived from a verb of hammering and stretching, not a verb of building or doming. That is a fact about the word's family tree. What the Hebrew is naming, in the most literal sense the lexicons allow, is *a hammered-out, stretched-flat, taut sheet.* A membrane. Under tension. Dividing something from something. Whether the ancient writer meant that image to carry forward into the reader's mind as literal architecture, or whether they meant it as a stylized figure, is a separate question — one the rest of this book will pursue. My narrower claim here is about the word itself: *raqia* is derived from a verb that names stretching, not doming, and a reader who encounters the word with that etymology in hand sees a stretched sheet before they see anything else.

One honest concession before I press on, because the etymology is not the only reading on the table. A major line of Old Testament scholarship — John Walton's *Genesis 1 as Ancient Cosmology* (2011) is the standard reference — reads *raqia* through the lens of ancient Near Eastern cosmography, where the sky was pictured as a solid dome over a flat earth, and argues that the ancient audience would have heard *raqia* as that dome rather than as an abstract stretched surface. That is the dominant alternative, and it is a serious one. I am not going to pretend I have refuted it. My claim is narrower than a verdict on what the ancient audience pictured: I am pointing at the verb the noun is built from, and noting that *raqa* names hammering-and-stretching, which is at least as live a thread in the word as doming — a thread the standard English translations preserve when they reach for *expanse* or *firmament*. Whether the stretched-sheet reading or the solid-dome reading is closer to the original intent is a question I will leave to the scholars who fight it out; what the rest of this book pursues is whether a physics exists that matches the stretched-sheet reading, and that is a different question from what the writer meant.

I want you to stop for a second, here, and stand with me in a workshop. Not a metaphorical one. An actual one. Watch a metalsmith with a bowl of thick gold wire and a small anvil hammer the wire, turn it, and hammer it again, until the wire is a sheet of foil so thin you can read a newspaper through it when you hold it up to the light. The foil is extremely thin — almost no apparent mass once it is this thin — yet the sheet is still *taut.* You can stretch it across a frame. You can put a drop of water on it and watch the drop sit there, held up by the surface tension of the sheet. That is the physical object the Hebrew verb *raqa* names. That is the object the noun *raqia* is derived from. A stretched, hammered-out, taut sheet that can do structural work.

When I first read the word this way — and I want to be honest with you, it was not in that kitchen-table moment; it was several years earlier, in a commentary I had ordered off a theology reading list because I had run out of aerospace papers on a Saturday afternoon — I sat up in my chair. I had spent the previous decade of my life working with stretched, taut, load-bearing sheets. The skin of an aircraft is one. The membrane of an aeroponic reservoir is another. The head of a drum in my workshop is one. The pressure-vessel diaphragm inside the hydraulic test rig at Insitu was one. I knew what a stretched membrane could do. I knew what it could not do. I knew the physics of stretched membranes well enough to be dangerous with it. And the document was telling me, in its own oldest vocabulary, that one of the load-bearing components of the system it was describing was a stretched membrane under tension.

That is not a metaphor I am imposing. That is the verb the noun comes from. If an English translator renders *raqia* as *firmament,* they have preserved the sense that the object is firm and structural. If they render it as *expanse,* they have preserved the sense that the object is spread out, extended. If they render it as *vault,* they have preserved the sense that the object is overhead. None of those English renderings preserves what the Hebrew carries natively, which is *a thing that has been hammered out and stretched.*

Circle the word. Circle it hard. The rest of this book is going to orbit around it.

---

A stretched membrane divides something from something. The document says so, in the same breath in which it names the membrane. *Let there be a raqia in the midst of the waters, and let it divide the waters from the waters.*

Which brings us to the fourth Hebrew word.

*Mayim* (מַיִם). The word English translators render as *waters.* Always plural — there is no singular form of *mayim* in Hebrew. Whatever the document is naming, it is naming something inherently multiple, not a single continuous fluid.

I need to be careful here, because this is the word that modern readers are most likely to misread. The misreading matters, because if you hear *waters* and picture the liquid in your kitchen sink, the whole architecture I am about to describe collapses into an obvious absurdity. There cannot have been kitchen-sink water before there was a universe for it to be in. So it is worth a minute to clear away what the ancient word does not mean, so that we can hear what it does.

In modern English, *waters* means H₂O. It means the chemical compound of two hydrogens and an oxygen, liquid at ordinary temperatures, found in oceans and rivers and your kitchen sink. Every Western reader over the age of ten has been trained, by a high-school chemistry class or its equivalent, to hear *waters* and picture H₂O.

The document of Genesis 1 is not using the word that way, and the reason it cannot be using the word that way is straightforward. In the passage where *mayim* first appears — the second verse of the first chapter — the text is describing a state of affairs in which, by its own explicit accounting, there are no stars, no sun, no planets, no chemistry, no molecules of any kind. The hydrogen-and-oxygen compound the modern reader pictures could not exist in the state the document is describing. Nothing the modern reader calls water exists yet. And yet the text uses *mayim.*

The way older Hebrew scholars — and careful modern ones — read this is that *mayim* in Genesis 1 is the word the ancient world used for an elemental fluid substance, something with the character of water in the sense of being a continuous flowing medium, but without the commitment that it is, chemically speaking, H₂O. The word names something primordial. Something that can flow, something that can be divided, something that can sit in a reservoir and be held apart from something else like it. What the document is telling an engineer reading the spec is that, before the membrane is stretched out, there exists this primordial medium; and after the membrane is stretched out, the medium has been divided into two reservoirs — *mayim* above the *raqia* and *mayim* below the *raqia* — held apart by the membrane.

Here is an analogy I can give you cleanly because I have built it, literally, with my own hands. If you have ever stood next to a two-tank aeroponic hydraulic system, you know what it looks like. I spent years designing and fixing those in a homestead workshop and later at Eden Grow Systems. Two reservoirs of fluid, different operating properties, held apart by a thin structure. Different pressures. Different flow rates. Sometimes, depending on how the system is configured, different working fluids entirely. The structure between them does the job of keeping them separate. You remove the structure and the two fluids equilibrate, which is almost never what you want. The structure is load-bearing; the fluids are what the structure holds apart.

That is the architecture the text is describing. A membrane, stretched under tension, dividing a primordial field into two reservoirs, one above and one below. I am not claiming the ancient writers had a hydraulic schematic in mind when they wrote the words. I am claiming that the words, read in their native grammar with their native meanings, describe that architecture — and that an engineer opening the document cold would circle the nouns, underline the verbs, and draw the following picture in the margin.

That is what the text says. Look at it. A three-part frame — *the heavens and the earth* as the whole system. A stretched membrane in the middle — the *raqia.* Two reservoirs on either side of the membrane, above and below — the *mayim.* Below the lower reservoir, dry ground that emerges when the lower waters *gather* — a fifth Hebrew word, *qavah* (קָוָה), which I will only name and not develop here; Chapter 9 deals with gathering and condensation. No zone numbers on the diagram. No physics notation. No Greek letters. Just what the document says.

I want to flag one thing about this figure before we move on, and I want to flag it in the voice I have been using all chapter. I am not yet claiming that this architecture matches the physics of the universe we live in. I am not yet claiming that the two reservoirs are the dark sectors. I am not yet claiming that the membrane is a physical membrane in any sense the contemporary physicist would accept. All I am claiming is that this is what the document describes when you read the Hebrew at its word. Whether there exists a physics that matches the architecture is the question the rest of this book is going to pursue. If there is, the document is doing something extraordinary. If there isn't, the architecture is interesting but empty, and I will tell you as much when we get there. Builder's honesty to the last. The diagram is not the framework. The diagram is the document.

---

I have spent most of this chapter on the individual words of Genesis 1. I want, before we close, to zoom out and show you the shape of the whole sequence — because the architecture the text describes is not only in the nouns and verbs but also in the order of operations. Genesis 1 is built around a seven-stage sequence, and the shape of that sequence matters.

I want to do something a little unusual, here, and ask you to suspend — for the length of the next two pages — every fight the Western church has had for the last hundred and fifty years about whether those seven stages are literal twenty-four-hour days, geological epochs, framework-hypothesis poetic categories, or phases of a continuous creative process. I have opinions on that question. I am not going to hand them to you in this chapter. The question of *how long* each stage took is a question about timescale. What I want to show you is a question about *structure* — what kind of system the sequence, as a sequence, describes.

Stage one. The opening act. Light is separated from darkness. A distinction between ordered and unordered states is established. A time arrow is set — there is an evening and there is a morning. An engineer reading this is looking at *initialization:* the system's basic temporal and informational machinery is set up. Zeros and ones. On and off. A place to put state.

Stage two. The *raqia* is stretched. The *mayim* are divided, above and below. The layered zone structure is installed. The membrane is placed in its middle. In engineering terms, this is the top-level *separation* operation — the moment when the architectural split that defines the rest of the system is actually carried out.

Stage three. The waters below *gather* (*qavah*) — that Hebrew verb I named a moment ago and am not developing here. Dry ground appears. The first life, plant life, begins. In engineering terms, *condensation and first biomass.* The fluid reservoir below the membrane condenses into matter that can hold its shape.

Stage four. The luminaries — sun, moon, stars — are set in their places. Their job, the text is explicit, is to serve as signs for seasons and days and years. *Time governance.* The system's clock is regulated against celestial markers.

Stage five. Aquatic and flying life. The first appearance, in the document, of the phrase *nephesh chayah* — often glossed as *living soul* or *living creature,* naming animate life. And here, for the first time since the opening sentence, the verb *bara* reappears. The document is telling you, in its own vocabulary, that a qualitatively new category has just been introduced. Not a fashioning. A bringing-into-existence of a new kind of thing.

Stage six. Land animals. Then, at the end of the stage, humans — and the verb *bara* is used three times in a single verse, the only place in the entire Hebrew Bible where that triple construction occurs. Whatever the document is doing on day six, the emphasis tells you it thinks the event is the most consequential in the sequence.

Stage seven. The system is declared complete. *Vayekhullu,* in the Hebrew — *were finished.* The operational input ceases. The document describes a state of rest, which — if you read it in its own terms — is not exhaustion. It is completion. It is the engineering equivalent of final acceptance: the system is done, the inputs can stop, the architecture is what it is going to be.

I have managed programs that looked like that sequence. I have sat in acceptance reviews where a system that was half-assembled six months earlier was signed off as complete, and the team could stop contributing new inputs and start living with what the architecture actually became. Every engineering lifecycle I have ever been part of has some version of that shape: initialization, separation, condensation, governance, populating, animating, completion. The words change. The shape is recognizable.

I want to be honest with you about what I am not doing and what I am doing in that last paragraph. I am *not* telling you that the seven stages of Genesis 1 each took six hours, or each took a billion years, or are purely figurative. I am not settling that question, because settling it is not what this chapter is for. I am *not* claiming that the Hebrew writers had a modern engineering lifecycle in mind when they wrote. I am claiming, more modestly, that the sequence the document lays out is structurally coherent — that it moves, in a recognizable way, from a featureless initial state through a series of specific operations to a completed system — and that this structural coherence is worth taking seriously before one decides whether to dismiss the document as pre-scientific. The full treatment of the seven-stage sequence and what the framework will say about it comes in Chapter 8. The open-and-shut question of timescale is one I will not pretend to have closed; I am skeptical of most people on both sides of the usual debate who claim to have closed it cleanly, and I will tell you more about why in Chapter 8.

So where does this leave us.

At the end of Chapter 1, I promised you that I would show you a document I had begun to read the way I read every other engineering document. I have now done that. What I wanted you to see, and what I hope you can see now, is not any particular theological conclusion. What I wanted you to see is that the document *reads.* Read as a specification, it describes a three-part frame — the heavens and the earth as the whole, with a stretched membrane in the middle and two reservoirs of primordial medium above and below. It uses two consistent verbs for two consistent categories of creative operation. It lays out a seven-stage build sequence that moves, recognizably, from initialization to completion. It names, specifically, load-bearing components and the operations performed on them. It is not poetry dressed up as specification. And — as far as I can tell from a decade of reading specifications for a living — when you apply the reading discipline to it, it reads the way specifications read.

What that *means* — whether there exists a physics that could actually instantiate that architecture, and whether such a physics would line up with the universe we live in, and whether its predictions would match the measurements we have made — is the question the rest of this book is going to pursue. I am not, in this chapter, asking you to believe that the architecture is real. I am asking you to sit with the fact that the document describes one.

The next chapter asks what happens if you actually try to build it.
