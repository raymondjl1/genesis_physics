# Chapter 10 — Why Gravity Pulls and Light Shines

## §1. The same hardware, doing two jobs

There is a specific view I carried around Hood River for most of a decade, and it is worth standing in before the physics starts.

A ScanEagle on a test stand. Small airframe, long slender wing, a nose full of sensors, an engine behind a prop, avionics packed into every cubic inch that will hold them. The drone is tied down to a fixture that can measure thrust and vibration. The engine is running. The radios are up. The spectrum analyzers on the bench are watching the airframe and the datalink and the GPS receiver and the telemetry link all at once.

What every engineer in that cell is doing, all day, every day, is managing two kinds of physics on the same piece of hardware simultaneously.

The first kind is gravity. The airframe has weight. The fuel has weight, and the weight changes as the fuel burns. The center of gravity shifts when the payload shifts. The thrust has to overcome weight to get off the rail, and the wing has to generate lift equal to weight to stay in the air. Gravity is not a topic we think about in that cell. It is a constant presence — a bookkeeping operation the world is doing on the airframe, every second, whether we are watching or not. The drone's existence is a long argument with gravity that we have to win every second it is airborne.

The second kind is electromagnetism. The radios are radiating. The datalink is pushing a carrier out of the airframe and punching through the test-cell walls to the ground station across the parking lot. The GPS receiver is picking up satellite signals from twelve thousand miles up — signals that are electromagnetic waves from birds orbiting in the dark over us. The avionics bus is running differential signaling at a specific impedance. The engine's ignition coil is kicking a spark across a plug gap that hammers the RF spectrum with broadband noise that the shielding is supposed to keep out of everything else. Electromagnetism is also not a topic we think about in that cell. It is also a constant presence — another bookkeeping operation the world is doing on the airframe, every second.

What the engineers in the cell do not do, ever, is treat those two kinds of physics as coming from two different worlds. They are two inputs to the same flight model. The drone is one object, and the world is doing two kinds of accounting on it at the same time.

That operator posture — *one object, two kinds of accounting* — is what I want to bring into the physics itself.

Because the claim the chapter is about to make is that this is not a coincidence of engineering. It is not that the drone happens to experience two separate force laws that the textbooks arrange on adjacent shelves. The claim is that the two kinds of accounting the world is doing on the airframe are two different views of the same underlying thing. Not an analogy. A mechanism. Gravity and electromagnetism are two behaviors of one substrate — the firmament, the stretched 4D membrane Chapter 4 laid out and Chapter 9 put particles on.

That is the chapter.

## §2. Two forces, two frameworks, no unification

Before I can claim a unification I need to name what the field actually has, without flinching.

Mainstream physics has two theories for the two most familiar forces, and they are two of the most precisely tested scientific theories in the history of the species.

General relativity, Einstein's 1915 theory, describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime under mass and energy. It predicts the perihelion precession of Mercury, the bending of starlight by the sun, the slowing of clocks in gravitational fields, the orbital decay of binary pulsars, the specific waveforms of gravitational waves from colliding black holes. Every one of those predictions has been tested, and every one has come in consistent with the theory. The field's internal joke is that if you want a gravitational measurement that disagrees with general relativity, you will have to invent a better experiment than anyone has invented yet.

Quantum electrodynamics, the Feynman-Schwinger-Tomonaga theory of the 1940s, describes electromagnetism at the quantum level as the exchange of photons between charged particles. It predicts the magnetic moment of the electron to more than ten decimal places of agreement with measurement — a precision that corresponds, roughly, to measuring the distance between Los Angeles and New York to within the width of a human hair. QED is the most precisely tested quantitative theory of anything, ever.

Both theories are real. Both are successful. Both match nature, separately, to extraordinary precision.

And they do not fit together.

The attempt to fuse them — to build a single mathematical theory in which gravity is quantized the way electromagnetism is quantized — runs into infinities in the quantum treatment of gravity that nobody has managed to renormalize. Every promising candidate produces divergent integrals that cannot be tamed by the standard techniques that work for QED. String theory has been the largest organized attempt at fusion for forty years and has produced a beautiful mathematical structure without yet producing a set of quantitative predictions that match nature better than the existing separate theories do. Loop quantum gravity, causal dynamical triangulations, asymptotic safety, twistor theory — each is a serious research program, each has made partial progress, and none has closed the problem. The field's shared understanding is that unification of gravity and the other forces, at the quantum level, is the single largest open problem in fundamental physics.

Einstein himself spent the last thirty years of his life on a smaller version of the same problem — a *classical* unification of gravity and electromagnetism, without the quantum piece. He did not succeed. His correspondence late in life is candid about it. He died in 1955 with the unification manuscript unfinished on the nightstand.

That is where the field is. Two theories, no fusion, a century-long shared problem. I am going to name that cleanly, because the chapter is about to make a specific claim and I do not want the reader to think the claim is larger than it is.

Here is the specific claim.

At the *classical* level — which is to say, at the level where general relativity and Maxwell's equations do their work, before quantum effects enter — gravity and electromagnetism share a substrate in this framework. Both force laws fall out of the same 6D action that Chapters 4, 6, and 9 have been walking around. Not as coincidence. As projections of a single mathematical object onto 4D physics.

That is a classical unification. It is what Einstein was reaching for in his last three decades. It is not quantum gravity — the framework's quantum-gravity work is ongoing and Book 1 does not summarize it — and it is not a unification with the strong and weak forces, which are Chapter 11 territory. It is the unification of the two forces this chapter is about, at the classical level the reader experiences them, from a single starting point.

This is the second payoff chapter of Book 1. Chapter 9 cashed the first check. This one cashes the second. The rest argues the mechanism through in plain English.

## §3. The trampoline, recalled

I need the controlling analogy one more time, because the chapter's whole job is to show two behaviors of the same medium, and the analogy has to carry both.

A trampoline, in a backyard, with the springs under tension and the fabric taut. The reader knows this object — pop-science books and PBS specials have used it as a stand-in for the fabric of spacetime for decades. Chapter 4 planted the trampoline. Chapter 9 used it lightly. This chapter makes it work harder.

What can a trampoline do?

Stand on it. The fabric dips under your feet. The dip has a shape — deeper right under you, shallower as you move out, dying off at the rim. It is a *local deformation* of the trampoline, forced by your weight and resisted by the trampoline's tension. Drop a bowling ball on it. The dip is deeper, the slopes steeper. If a marble happened to be sitting on the trampoline near the bowling ball, it would not sit still. It would roll along the slope of the dip, downhill, toward the ball. The bowling ball has not pulled on the marble through any mysterious action at a distance. The bowling ball has changed the *shape* of the surface the marble is sitting on, and the marble, following the new shape, rolls downhill.

That is the first thing the trampoline does. It carries a local curvature, forced by what is sitting on it, and other things on the trampoline follow the curvature.

The second thing it does is different.

Leave the trampoline empty again. Tap the edge — a sharp knock with the back of your hand, or a coin dropped on the fabric near the rim. A ripple runs across the surface. It propagates away from where you tapped, crosses the trampoline, reaches the opposite edge, and either reflects or dies in the springs. The ripple does not sit anywhere. It travels. It moves at a speed set by the trampoline's physical properties — tension and mass per unit area. Tighten the springs, it goes faster. Loosen them, it goes slower. Swap the fabric for something heavier, it slows down. The speed of the ripple is the square root of tension divided by density — the standard wave-speed formula any introductory physics class runs through in the first week for any stretched medium.

The speed of a wave on a stretched medium is not a parameter you pick. It is forced by the medium. The medium cannot support a wave at any other speed.

Chapter 4 stated this in words for the firmament. The firmament is a stretched 4D membrane with a specific tension and a specific mass per unit area, and disturbances on it propagate at the square root of tension over density. *That wave speed is what we measure as the speed of light.* Two hundred and ninety-nine million, seven hundred and ninety-two thousand, four hundred and fifty-eight meters per second — not a fundamental constant inserted into the theory, but the wave speed of the firmament, derivable in principle from the membrane's tension and density.

So the trampoline has two behaviors. It dips under weight. It carries waves. Both come out of the same object, both are forced by the trampoline's physical properties. Same is true of the firmament. Those two behaviors are what this chapter matches to gravity and electromagnetism.

The trampoline analogy has limits. A trampoline is 2D; the firmament is 4D. A trampoline has no equivalent of the waters-above condensate Chapter 9 introduced, so a trampoline's "particles" have no rest mass mechanism. A trampoline has no off-diagonal geometric structure; the gauge structure electromagnetism relies on has no analog in a backyard trampoline. The trampoline is a metaphor for the architecture, not a miniature of it. When the analogy breaks I will say so.

With that on the table, the chapter can move to the physics.

## §4. Gravity as the membrane bending under weight

Put the image together.

The firmament is the stretched 4D membrane from Chapter 4. Sitting on it are the standing-wave patterns Chapter 9 identified as particles — localized patterns with mass, where mass is the geometric overlap of the pattern with the waters-above condensate. Each pattern has energy density, continuously, everywhere the pattern lives. That energy density presses on the firmament — not as an analogy, not as a way of speaking, but mechanically, the way a bowling ball on a trampoline presses on the fabric.

The membrane's tension resists the press, and the result is a specific curvature profile around the pattern. Deep near the pattern. Shallower with distance. Dying off at a rate set by the geometry of how a 4D membrane curves around a point source.

A neighboring pattern on the firmament, sitting some distance away, feels that curvature as a local slope. The membrane is not flat where the neighbor sits; it is tilted, bending toward the first pattern. The neighbor, like a marble on a dipped trampoline, follows the slope. Its natural motion — what it would do if the membrane were flat — is modified by the fact that the membrane is not flat. The neighbor drifts along the curvature, toward the first pattern.

*That drift is what we experience as gravitational attraction.*

Stated plainly. Gravity is not a force in the Newtonian sense of one object reaching across empty space to pull on another. Gravity is the shape of the membrane. Each pattern presses on the membrane, curving it locally. Each other pattern on the curved membrane drifts along the curvature toward its neighbors. The total motion of patterns, aggregated, is what the human-sized world calls the gravitational pull of one object on another.

The inverse-square law of Newtonian gravity — the law every high-school physics student memorizes — comes out of the geometry of how a membrane curves around a point source in three spatial dimensions and how the curvature's slope falls off with distance. Newton's law is the weak-field limit of this picture. Foundations Volume 2, Chapter 2 (*Gravity*) carries the derivation — it starts from the 6D action and the membrane's tension, writes down the equation that governs how the membrane curves around a localized pattern, and shows that the far-field behavior matches Newton's law with a specific numerical value for the gravitational constant G.

At larger scales, near black holes, around neutron stars, out at the global geometry of the universe, the weak-field picture breaks down. The membrane's curvature near a massive pattern is not a small dip anymore; it is a dramatic local deformation. At those scales the full picture is the one Einstein wrote down in 1915, which has become famous under the name *Einstein's field equations* — a set of ten coupled partial differential equations that govern how the membrane's 4D geometry responds to whatever matter and energy are riding on it.

This is the first named object of the chapter, and per the rule I am working under, I name it and do not write it. Einstein's field equations are what the framework recovers at the 4D projection of the 6D action for the gravitational sector of the membrane's behavior. Foundations Volume 2, Chapter 8 (*Gravitational Field Theory*) and Volume 5, Chapter 1 (*Einstein Field Equations*) carry the derivation — they start from the 6D action, integrate over the extra dimensions the way Chapter 6 described, and produce Einstein's field equations as the 4D piece that governs the membrane's shape. The reader who wants the mathematical detail goes to those two Foundations chapters. The reader following Book 1 gets the mechanism: the equations that govern how spacetime curves under matter and energy fall out of this framework as the equations that govern how the firmament curves under standing-wave patterns on it.

## §5. Light as the membrane vibrating

Now the second behavior.

Leave the gravity picture for a moment. No bowling ball. The trampoline empty. The firmament unweighted, flat, at rest.

Flick it. Disturb the membrane somewhere — accelerate a charged standing-wave pattern sitting on it, or strike the membrane with any disturbance that does not settle into a localized pattern. The disturbance does not stay put. It moves. A ripple propagates across the membrane, away from where it was made, at the membrane's wave speed — the square root of tension over mass density, the same formula any stretched medium obeys.

For the firmament, that speed is 299,792,458 meters per second. The speed of light.

That is the second behavior of the trampoline. Waves propagate across it at a forced speed. On the firmament, the forced speed is *c*.

A photon, in this framework, is one such traveling wave on the firmament. A specific disturbance of what physicists call the off-diagonal components of the 6D metric — the piece of the geometry that couples the ordinary spatial directions of the membrane to the extra-dimensional axes, which Chapter 6 laid out structurally — that carries energy and momentum across the membrane at *c*.

What a photon is *not*, in this framework, is a little ball flying through space. What a photon is *not* is a particle sitting on the membrane the way an electron does. A photon is something the membrane is *doing*. A wave, traveling, at the wave speed of the medium. The photon has no rest mass because it has no localized part of itself that has to sit still; there is nothing for the waters-above condensate to couple to, because the photon is entirely a propagating disturbance of the membrane itself. That is why the photon moves at *c* and not some slower speed. If the photon had any reach into the waters above, any overlap with the condensate, it would have rest mass and it would move slower. It does not. It moves at the wave speed of the medium it is a disturbance of. Chapter 9 introduced this; Chapter 10 uses it.

The full classical theory of what happens when the firmament vibrates is the theory every undergraduate electrical engineer has memorized under a specific name. *Maxwell's equations.* Four coupled partial differential equations, written down by James Clerk Maxwell in the 1860s, that govern how electric fields and magnetic fields behave in space and time.

Named. Not written. The four laws, by name:

Gauss's law for electricity — electric fields diverge from regions of positive charge and converge on regions of negative charge.

Gauss's law for magnetism — there are no magnetic monopoles; magnetic field lines close on themselves.

Faraday's law — a time-varying magnetic flux through a surface generates an electromotive force around the boundary of the surface, which is how every electric generator in the world produces power.

Ampère-Maxwell law — an electric current, plus a time-varying electric field, generates a magnetic field, which is the reason radios transmit and inductors work.

Those four laws, combined with the constitutive relations between fields and matter, describe every classical electromagnetic phenomenon there is: static electric fields, steady currents, radio waves, light, microwaves, the operation of every generator and motor and transformer ever built, every antenna, every fiber-optic cable. Engineering electromagnetism runs on Maxwell's equations, every day, in every piece of hardware I have ever worked with.

The framework recovers all four of Maxwell's equations from the 6D action. Foundations Volume 2, Chapter 3 (*Electromagnetism*) and Volume 2, Chapter 7 (*Classical E&M*) carry the derivation. The 6D action contains a gauge piece — the part of the membrane's mathematical description that governs its off-diagonal metric components — and when that action is projected from 6D down to 4D by integrating over the extra dimensions, the resulting 4D equations *are* Maxwell's equations. Not analogs of Maxwell's equations. Not something that looks like Maxwell's equations in some limit. Exactly the four equations Maxwell wrote down in the 1860s, falling out as the gauge piece of the 4D projection.

Along with Maxwell's equations, the framework recovers several named consequences that the reader probably knows individually. The electromagnetic wave equation — the statement that electric and magnetic fields satisfy a wave equation in vacuum, with wave speed *c* — comes out as the direct consequence of taking Maxwell's equations in a charge-free region. Coulomb's law — the inverse-square law for the force between static electric charges — comes out as the static limit of Gauss's law. It is the statement that the wave speed of the membrane is the same thing as the speed at which electromagnetic disturbances propagate on it — because they are the same disturbances. To be precise about what is and is not new here: standard Lorentz-covariant electrodynamics also produces this identity, so the identity itself is not the framework's contribution. The framework's added claim is that *c* is the wave speed of a specific physical membrane — that the identity has a mechanical reason rather than being a postulate built into the structure of the equations.

Gauge invariance — the deep symmetry of electromagnetism that says you can shift the electromagnetic potentials in a specific mathematical way without changing the physical fields — comes out of the framework as the reparameterization freedom in the extra-dimensional coordinates. When you integrate over the extra dimensions, there is freedom in how you choose the coordinates on those dimensions, and that freedom manifests in 4D as gauge freedom.
