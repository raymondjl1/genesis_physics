## §1. The IRT and the bookkeeping

There is a table I sat at for the better part of a year that I want to put the reader at, before the physics starts.

The program was a multi-billion-dollar intelligence satellite. The bird was real hardware, assembled, integrated, about six months out from a launch window the program office did not want to miss. What a lot of people on the outside of that kind of review do not understand is what the IRT is actually *doing* all day. It is not debating doctrine. It is checking that the bird's conservation laws close out under every failure mode anyone around the table can imagine.

Bus power is a conservation law. Every watt the arrays produce is either used by a load, stored in a battery, or dissipated as heat. The ledger has to balance in every mode — safe, nominal, sun-pointing, across an eclipse season, during a payload dump. The bus engineer asks one question across them all: under what off-nominal condition does this ledger stop closing. Thermal is a conservation law — every joule the bird absorbs, produces, or emits has to balance. Momentum is a conservation law — the reaction wheels store angular momentum and the thrusters dump it, and the GNC engineer is watching a year-long budget and asking where it breaks.

The whole review is this. Around the table, across the days, across the ledgers. Conservation laws as the load-bearing clauses of the flightworthiness argument.

Nobody at that table is treating conservation laws as ornamental. Nobody is saying *well, energy is usually conserved.* The laws are real the way the bolt holding the star tracker to the optical bench is real. If the ledger does not close, the bird does not fly. Full stop.

I sat through enough of those reviews to absorb, at the level of professional reflex, what conservation laws actually are in operator practice. They are not postulates. They are not conveniences. ## §3. The engine, and Noether's theorem

Imagine an engine on a stand.

Not a metaphor. A specific thing. A small four-stroke on a test fixture, or a single-cylinder piston engine in a development cell, or — for the reader who has ever tuned a radio front-end — an RF oscillator with its timing chain open on the bench. Something the author has actually worked on. Something the reader has at least driven or seen run.

The engineer tuning the engine does not, consciously, think *the engine is time-translation invariant.* But the whole craft assumes it. The engine does not care what absolute time its next cycle starts at. Run it at nine in the morning, run it at nine at night, run it tomorrow — same behavior, as long as the inputs are the same. Intervals between events matter; absolute time does not. The same applies to space: the engine runs the same whether the fixture is in Hood River, Oregon or in Huntsville, Alabama. The engineer does not write these indifferences down. They are absorbed into professional reflex. But each one is a symmetry of the engine's physics, and each one has a consequence.

In 1918 a German mathematician named Emmy Noether proved a theorem. Noether is a figure who deserves more public recognition than she has, and I will not spend a paragraph explaining why. The theorem is what matters here. Translated into plain English: *every continuous symmetry of a system's action produces a conserved quantity.* Time-translation symmetry of the action produces the conservation of energy. Space-translation symmetry produces the conservation of momentum. Rotation symmetry produces the conservation of angular momentum. Gauge symmetry — a more abstract kind of symmetry, involving the internal relabeling of fields — produces the conservation of charge.

Noether proved it as a theorem. It has been on the books for a century. It is the backbone of modern theoretical physics. Every graduate student in the field reproduces it; every textbook contains it. No framework that wants to be taken seriously replaces it, and this one does not try. The framework inherits Noether's theorem.

What the framework adds is the observation that the symmetries Noether's theorem wants — the ones that produce the conservation laws — are themselves geometric properties of the 6D action the chapter has been walking around for ten chapters.

The membrane's tension does not depend on the absolute time coordinate. That is time-translation symmetry of the 6D action. Noether gives energy conservation from it.

The membrane's structure does not depend on the absolute spatial coordinate. That is space-translation symmetry of the 6D action. Noether gives momentum conservation from it.

The 6D action is rotation-invariant in the appropriate way — the membrane's physics looks the same whether you stand on your head or not, in the technical sense of being invariant under spatial rotations. Noether gives angular-momentum conservation from it.

The gauge piece of the 6D action — the one Chapter 10 identified as producing Maxwell's equations at the 4D projection — carries a specific reparameterization freedom. The framework's version of gauge symmetry. Noether gives charge conservation from it.

Four symmetries. Four conservation laws. One theorem. All four symmetries are geometric properties of the architecture — not postulates, not conveniences, not things we put in to make the math work. This is not a hand-wave. Chapter 3 established the zone manifold's structure; Chapter 6 specified the 6D embedding geometry. For Book 1's purposes, the reader may trust the claim; the derivation lives in Foundations.

This is the payoff of the chapter's first move. The conservation laws the reader has carried since high school as "rules the universe follows" are the architecture's invariances made visible. The engine on the stand is the operator's picture of the same logic — the hardware's invariances force what is conserved. The framework says the same is true of the cosmos as hardware.

## §4. The first and zeroth laws

Thermodynamics is the second rule family, and the first, zeroth, and third laws are the easy ones to handle. None of them is disrupted by the framework. All three survive the open-system widening Chapter 5 established with their local statements unchanged. The second law is the hard one, and it gets its own section.

The **zeroth law** says that if two bodies are each in thermal equilibrium with a third body, they are in equilibrium with each other. In plain English: temperature is a well-defined thing. The reason mattering to thermodynamics classes and to refrigeration engineers in equal measure is that this law is what lets us put numbers on temperature in the first place. If thermal equilibrium were not transitive, temperature would not be a scalar quantity; we could not assign a number to "how hot" a body is. The zeroth law guarantees we can.

The framework does not touch the zeroth law. In the architecture's terms, it is a statement about how the membrane's standing-wave patterns — the patterns Chapter 9 identified as particles — exchange energy with each other and reach local equilibria. The pattern-mode structure of the 6D action produces exactly the zeroth-law behavior. The reader can hold the zeroth law as they learned it. Foundations Volume 3, Chapter 9 (*Four Laws of Thermodynamics*) writes out the zone-architecture version.

The **first law** says energy is conserved. In the thermodynamic form familiar from college physics: the change in a system's internal energy equals the heat added to the system minus the work the system does on its surroundings. In one line, plain: energy neither appears from nothing nor vanishes into nothing; it only moves across a system's boundary and between its internal forms.

The first law is the Noether-energy-conservation statement from section 3, stated in the thermodynamicist's vocabulary. Same physical content. Different accounting language.

The textbook statement is exactly correct inside every domain the reader has ever measured — lab, star, galaxy, visible universe on timescales where the open-system flux can be treated as constant. The framework does not revise the first law's local statement at any scale the reader has ever encountered.

Where Chapter 5 widened the frame was at the cosmic scale as a whole. Energy flows continuously from the waters-above reservoir across the sustaining coupling into the firmament — the dark-energy-labeled contribution to the cosmological constant. At the scale of the cosmos taken together with the reservoir and the coupling, the accounting is strict: the *cosmos-plus-reservoir-plus-coupling* is energetically conservative. Inside the cosmos alone, the accounting has a source term a closed-system framing would miss.

The working engineer has always had this right. Nobody analyzing an automobile engine forgets the fuel tank and the exhaust when budgeting the engine's energy. Every undergraduate heat-transfer class teaches the student to draw the control volume correctly. The framework is doing the same exercise at cosmic scale. The first law, stated correctly — "energy is conserved in a closed system" — is unchanged. The framework just makes explicit which system you have to close. Foundations Volume 3, Chapter 9 carries the full accounting.

The **third law** says absolute zero cannot be reached in a finite number of steps. ## §5. The second law, and the arrow of time

The second law is the hardest of the four laws to state carefully, because it carries two claims the reader has probably heard fused into one sentence: *entropy always increases, and that is why the universe is running down toward heat death.* The second clause does not follow from the first without an assumption. ## §6. Causality and the speed-of-light limit

The last rule family. Causality — effects do not precede their causes — and its concrete consequence, the speed-of-light limit on how fast any influence in the cosmos can propagate.

The reader has learned, since some point in high school, that nothing goes faster than light. The reader has probably learned it with the implicit framing that *c* is an arbitrary cosmic speed limit built into the structure of spacetime — a number nature happens to enforce, for reasons never quite explained. Einstein's 1905 special relativity required the limit and derived its consequences. General relativity, in 1915, rebuilt gravity around it. Modern physics takes the limit as axiomatic.

The framework's reading is different. *c* is not an arbitrary speed limit. It is the wave speed of a specific physical object — the firmament — and the speed-of-light limit is the statement that the cosmos is made of the firmament's disturbances, and a disturbance of a medium cannot outrun the medium.

Recall Chapter 4. The firmament is a stretched 4D membrane with a specific tension and a specific mass density. Disturbances on it — ripples, waves, pattern deformations — propagate at a wave speed that is the square root of tension divided by mass density. For the firmament, that wave speed works out to 299,792,458 meters per second. Plus or minus nothing, in vacuum, because the speed of light in vacuum is one of the quantities physics now defines rather than measures. That number is what the reader calls the speed of light.

Chapter 4 stated this in words. Chapter 10 used it for the unification of gravity and electromagnetism — gravity as curvature of the membrane, electromagnetism as propagating waves of the membrane's gauge piece, both at *c* because both are behaviors of the same membrane. This chapter uses it again, for causality.

Here is the observation the framework hangs causality on. Everything in the cosmos is, in the architecture, a disturbance of the firmament in some way. A particle is a standing-wave pattern (Chapter 9). A photon is a traveling wave (Chapter 10). A gravitational influence is a propagating curvature (Chapter 10). A signal through any medium — sound through air, an electrical pulse through a wire, a biochemical signal through a nerve — is ultimately particle motions and electromagnetic field changes, both disturbances of the firmament. Nothing in the cosmos is made of something other than the firmament's disturbances. That claim is the cumulative content of the ten chapters before this one.

A disturbance of a medium cannot outrun the medium. This is a straightforward fact about waves: if a trampoline's waves travel at a certain speed, nothing made of the trampoline's waves can propagate faster. The firmament's waves propagate at *c*. Therefore, nothing made of the firmament's disturbances can propagate at more than *c*. The speed-of-light limit is an architectural statement, not a postulated one. The substrate has a maximum propagation speed. Everything the substrate can do inherits that limit.

Causality follows directly. A cause at point A can influence point B only if a disturbance can propagate from A to B; the fastest any disturbance propagates is *c*; so point B cannot be affected any sooner than A-to-B divided by *c*. Effects cannot precede their causes, not by convention, but because the substrate cannot carry them fast enough.

The regions of spacetime that can be reached from a given event — at speeds up to *c* — form what physicists call the future light cone of the event. The regions of spacetime from which the event could have been reached form the past light cone. Outside both cones is the region no causal influence can bridge. The reader has seen these diagrams in popular relativity books. The framework does not disagree about the diagrams; the framework reads them as the architecture's speed limit drawn in 4D. The light cone is the membrane's maximum propagation speed, pictured.

Foundations Volume 5, Chapter 1 (*Einstein Field Equations*) carries the rigorous derivation of the causal structure from the metric of the 6D action. The relativistic light cone falls out as a theorem, not as an axiom. Everything Einstein's 1905 paper required of special relativity — the invariance of *c*, the frame-independence of the causal structure, the specific form of the Lorentz transformations — follows from treating *c* as the membrane's wave speed. Einstein's move, in 1905, was to hold the speed-of-light limit as a principle and see what physics demanded. The framework is doing the inverse: identifying the substrate whose wave speed that limit *is*, and letting the principle fall out as a consequence.

One forward flag, one sentence. Chapter 12 will take up specific zone-boundary cases where the *local* wave speed of the firmament differs from the nominal *c* — regions where the membrane has different local physical properties, and therefore a different local wave speed. Those are not violations of causality. They are places where causality still holds with respect to the local wave speed, and the framework predicts testable departures from the nominal-*c* picture. This chapter is staying with the nominal case: the framework predicts *c* as the global firmament wave speed, and relativistic causality as its architectural consequence, and Chapter 12 takes up where the framework predicts the exceptions.
